#2. Sri Lankan turbine project fuels anger as fish hauls plunge

The project has been blamed for a host of problems, from dwindling catches to floods, fuelling opposition to green energy projects as fuel costs soar.

This photograph taken on Oct 3, 2026 shows a fisherman working on fishing nets at a beach near a wind power plant in Sri Lanka's mannar peninsula. (Photo: AFP/Ishara S Kodikara)

SRI LANKA: Beneath rotating wind turbines on Sri Lanka's Mannar peninsula, a fishing net is hauled up the beach by tractor winches and 20 men, but once again the catch is meagre.

"I've been fishing here since I was a boy," said Venkatasamy Kalimuththu, 65, looking disappointedly at the catch - a few good fish, some small fry, and several clumps of seaweed.

"Before the wind turbines were installed, we caught a lot of fish," he added. "Now, much less."

Since they appeared six years ago, the Mannar wind turbines have been blamed for a host of problems - from dwindling catches to floods, fuelling opposition to green energy projects as fuel costs soar.

The opposition comes as war in the Middle East sends fuel and electricity costs soaring, making the shift to renewable energy an economic imperative.

Although he admits he has no proof, Kalimuththu is convinced the spinning blades are driving fish away.

"When the fans rotate, the shadow falls on the water," he said. "So the fish don't swim up to the shallows."

His colleagues agree, also blaming the turbines - as well as competition from Indian trawlers.

"We are not opposed to renewable energy in principle," said Lesley Jesurajan, a leading figure in the local civil society group OPEnE. "But not here. There are plenty of other places to install wind turbines."

Owned by state utility Ceylon Electricity Board (CEB), the turbines line a narrow strip of sand exposed to winds sweeping across the gulf between Sri Lanka and India.

The first phase - 30 turbines with a combined capacity of 104 megawatts - joined the grid in 2020, and construction on another 14 has begun.

A third phase of 52 turbines had been awarded to Indian conglomerate Adani, but the government cancelled the contract in 2025 after a dispute over the price per kilowatt-hour.

Benedict Mary Vijiliya's village, around 100m from the coast, is surrounded by wind turbines.

She claims concrete foundations and trenches for access roads have prevented rainwater from draining towards the sea.

"During the monsoon rains, the water comes up to our waists and the toilets overflow," complains Vijiliya, president of the Women's Rural Development Society (WRDS), showing photographs on her phone of her home submerged under about a metre of water.

"We never had experienced that before 2020. Our homes are damaged, our cattle and our crops are affected, our livelihood is impacted and we can't even drink the water of our well anymore".

In neighbouring Pesalai, fishing incomes have plummeted, said A Stanislas Peries, head of the local fishermen's cooperative.

"Before, people could earn up to 5,000 rupees (US$15) a day from fishing. Now they barely make more than 1,000. Things have become very difficult."

Energy Minister Anura Karunathilaka said he had heard the complaints.

"We are carrying out feasibility studies," he said. "Once we implement this ... the flood situation they are currently facing can be reduced."

The government aims to generate 70 per cent of the country's electricity from renewable sources by 2030, including wind power. But it has frozen new projects in Mannar to ease local opposition.

"We have already pledged ... that only those projects already approved will be carried out."

Father S Marcus, a Catholic priest and head of the Mannar Citizens' Committee, remains unconvinced.

"They had several times promised mitigation measures but nothing happened ... they are cheating our people," he added.

At his initiative, residents have filed two complaints in Sri Lankan courts. But a separate one has been filed against him.

"Apparently, we are disrupting the government," he said. "But we will not back down - we will put an end to all this."

Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, the Workers' Party (PT) candidate for presidential re-election, greets supporters outside his residence on the day of the presidential runoff election, in Sao Paulo, Brazil on October 25, 2026. | Photo Credit: Reuters

Brazil's leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva pulled off a dramatic comeback on Sunday (October 25, 2026) as he clinched election victory against right-wing senator Flavio Bolsonaro, who trounced him in the first round.

The leftist icon, who turns 81 on Tuesday (October 27), defied all projections to win an unprecedented fourth term at the helm of Latin America's largest country.

According to official results, he won 51.75% of the votes to 48.25% for Flavio Bolsonaro, son of former far-right President Jair Bolsonaro, who had entered election day confident with his lead in the polls.

With Mr. Lula's victory, Brazil staved off a conservative wave that has seen numerous countries — such as Argentina, Chile, Colombia, and Peru — all shift right, backed by U.S. President Donald Trump.

After a campaign marred by scandal and mud-slinging, Sunday's (October 25) vote plunged into chaos after a last-minute Supreme Court ruling extending voting by two hours, at the request of Mr. Lula who said buses had been pulled from circulation in various cities, barring citizens from voting.

Flavio Bolsonaro also requested an extension in some parts of the country due to heavy rains, but his camp lashed out at the country-wide extension.

"Either we win. Or we win. Consider yourself warned," pro-Bolsonaro lawmaker Nikolas Ferreira wrote on X as the votes streamed in.

The bitter campaign laid bare the deep divisions in the country of over 200 million people.

"It's a disgrace. We've never had anything like this in Brazil, and now they've come up with the idea of extending the election period by two hours," said Aurelio Junior, 45, a dentist in Brasilia.

Jacqueline Maria Lima de Franca, 34, a business owner in Rio de Janeiro backing Mr. Lula said it was "more than fair...everyone should have the right to vote, and that was being neglected."

Mr. Lula, a defining figure in Brazilian politics over four decades, was blindsided after his first-round defeat to Mr. Bolsonaro.

The left swung into action with massive rallies declaring "Never Again Bolsonaro," where protesters recalled the former president's chaotic handling of the Covid pandemic, devastation of the Amazon rainforest and cuts to public programs.

Mr. Lula also revamped his social media strategy, taking video selfies and even bought his first cellphone, while supporters carried out a feverish campaign to change the minds of family, friends and neighbours, and encourage them to vote after high levels of abstention.

His re-election marks another remarkable comeback for the man who grew up dirt poor in the northeast in a family of illiterate farmers.

Mr. Lula, revered for social welfare programs that lifted millions out of poverty, made a dramatic return to office in 2022 elections after a corruption scandal saw him jailed for over 18 months.

The charges, annulled on procedural grounds, have continued to haunt him.

Supporters of Brazil's President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, the Workers' Party (PT) candidate for re-election, react to early result during the presidential runoff election, in Sao Paulo, Brazil on October 25, 2026. | Photo Credit: Reuters

In his third term, he has overseen a drop in inflation and unemployment, and lifted millions out of hunger, but voters complain about the cost of living and a sense of insecurity.

Mr. Lula's social policies became a point of tension as he expanded welfare programs during the campaign.

"Brazil needs more time with Lula, primarily because the country is on the verge of falling into the clutches of fascism," Maria Fernanda Elisbao, a 21-year-old editorial assistant, told AFP.

Mr. Lula heads into a fourth term with a country divided, with almost half of the nation voting against him and desperate for change.

"We're in a deep hole, things are dire. People can barely afford to eat — buying meat is out of the question," said Tereza Cristina Ramos, a 68-year-old retiree, who voted in Rio de Janeiro.

Already one of the world's oldest leaders, Mr. Lula will be 85 by the end of his new term in 2030.

He will thus have spent 16 years in power, following his first stint as President from 2003 to 2010.

Mr. Lula is also facing a hostile Congress dominated by the right.

His options are also constrained by the economic outlook, with growth expected to slow.

He has also come under renewed pressure to confront rampant insecurity in a country plagued by criminal factions that sow terror in working-class neighbourhoods.

The President's campaign leaned heavily on the theme of sovereignty in the face of commercial and diplomatic pressure from Mr. Trump's United States.

Voters must "choose a country where the flag is respected," he said, his raspy voice more hoarse than ever after a tireless campaign. He accused his rival of wanting to "govern with the American flag."

#9. A shark went astray and captured Korea's heart - will she finally make it back to sea?

Watch: BBC follows operation to rescue shark stranded in South Korean canal

As soon as a large shark was spotted in a canal in Busan - a bustling shipping hub and beach city on South Korea's south-eastern tip - marine biologist Kim Hyun-woo was drafted in to rescue it.

He got the call in mid-September, on his way home from a business trip tracking shoals of mackerel to aid fishing vessels. Despite being one of the country's leading shark experts, he never thought he'd be asked by the coastguard to actually save one.

South Korea is no stranger to sharks but one this size, in a shallow, concrete canal right by the world's seventh busiest port, was unheard of - until now.

With an excited crowd already gathering at the waterfront, his team from the National Institute of Fisheries Science had to move fast.

The first task was to determine the species.

Some sharks are more dangerous than others. The star of the 1975 blockbuster Jaws, the great white shark, is known to attack and kill humans.

The specimen in Busan was found to be a female dusky shark, a species linked only to two deadly attacks. Still, Kim feared someone in the crowd might jump or fall into the canal.

The dusky shark was first spotted in the canal in mid-September

A small coastguard ship made an initial attempt using an electrical deterrence device, which generates a weak current that sharks avoid.

They're usually used after sightings near popular beaches. But after five hours, officials determined this particular shark was too large and fast for the device.

Kim changed tack, deciding instead to wait for the shark to let herself out. But that did not happen.

As authorities looked for ways to guide her out of the canal, the stranded creature went viral.

Just hours after she appeared, the council had sent everyone in the city an emergency text alert, urging pedestrians and residents to "exercise caution for their safety".

People rarely visited the park along the canal but because of the message, the place was soon brimming with people eager to spot the shark.

Adoring fans named her Bukangi, from "buk hang", the Korean name of the nearby harbour. Within days, a business trademarked it and Bukangi pins, plushies and T-shirts began selling online.

City authorities joined in, livestreaming the shark and making her their official mascot.

A banner was hung at the entrance of the canal, asking the public to sign a petition "on behalf of Bukangi" to demand the national government's support for the construction of a new 30,000-seater baseball stadium by the canal.

Shark spotting became a team effort among locals and visitors. The unspoken rule was that children were given the front row, and whoever saw Bukangi had to point her out to newer arrivals.

Some self-appointed defenders even patrolled the canal at night to make sure no-one was shining torches or lasers at Bukangi.

A poster encourages support for a baseball stadium "on behalf of Bukangi"

More than 1.6 million visitors ultimately flocked to the canal.

The local mayor said the shark brought "the kind of publicity even billions of dollars cannot buy" to the port.

"It's really rare these days for people to come together," said Kim Joo-eun, who came to see the shark with her two nieces. "I feel that Bukangi brought us all together, cheering for herâ¦ It's a lot of fun."

But officials couldn't leave Bukangi in the canal.

As September drew to an end, she hadn't been seen feeding for days and was spotted with bloody abrasions on her snout from bumping into the concrete walls.

Prominent marine biology professor Choi Yoon told local media that experts were particularly worried about a "critical" injury around the shark's eye.

Kim Joo-eun and her nieces were among Bukangi's many visitors

At the end of September, Kim's team mounted another rescue attempt. The marine biologist looked back to a 2004 incident in Massachusetts, when water jets were used to drive a great white shark away from a shallow pond.

But when the team tried this on Bukangi, she kept circling back.

On 2 October, they decided to install a net spanning the width of the canal to stop her from returning. She managed to swim around the net and was back within hours.

At this point, Kim started thinking the shark might have problems with her snout's electromagnetic sensors, which she uses for navigation. Or she might have become confused by the loud noises emitted by ships in the harbour.

The reason Bukangi was even in the canal may be a sign of warming seas, the oceans ministry suggests.

It believes she may have followed plentiful fish to warmer waters. Some 50 large sharks were recorded off South Korea's east coast in the first half of the year; four times as many as last year.

The canal leading up to the Busan port where Bukangi was stranded

With each failed attempt some of Bukangi's newfound fans began demanding she be left in peace.

On Thursday, several showed up with placards and shouted "leave Bukangi alone" and "remove the net".

When a security guard told them to stop, one man cried: "I love you, Bukangi!"

"I am obsessed with her," said Jeong Yo-won, a woman in her 40s. She called the shark a "source of hope and joy" and insisted, "I don't think we should evict her mercilessly like this."

Jeong protesting against Bukangi's "eviction", alongside a man demanding officials "protect Bukangi's right to self-determination"

Others thought it was time for her to return home.

"I would stare at her for hours because I was so enamoured by her," a woman said after sighting the shark on her third attempt. "I hope Bukangi would stay, but I prefer that she lives a long and healthy life."

People joked the council should "make an official ID for Bukangi and give a pension", Shin Seungmin said.

"She brought so much fun with her. But she should go and live her normal shark life."

Bukangi became a sensation, drawing the curious and the concerned

Staying in the canal would seriously jeopardise Bukangi because of falling autumn temperatures, Kim explains. Usually this prompts sharks to migrate to warmer waters further south.

"This canal is far from something we can call a habitat," he adds. "A wild animal cannot stay in a space made strictly for humans."

Eventually, as public scrutiny grew, the oceans ministry took over the rescue operation from the fisheries institute. Around 40 personnel, including divers from Busan's aquarium, were mobilised. A vet was on standby, along with an ambulance in case the divers were attacked by Bukangi.

On Wednesday divers installed two layers of nets, and Bukangi was successfully herded out of the narrow canal into open water. But overnight, she swam back into the canal, diving under the outer net.

This left her trapped between the two layers.

Then on thursday, rescuers removed the outer layer, and Bukangi was seen swimming further and further towards open sea.

Although she did occasionally circle back, the rescue operation had ended.

Staying in the canal is dangerous for Bukangi, says marine biologist Kim Hyun-woo

There have been no sightings of Bukangi in the last few days, which experts say is a positive sign.

Prof Choi believes she may be adjusting to wider, deeper waters.

She is unlikely to have travelled far, he adds, and could reappear over the coming week.

But he is also worried about any injuries she may have sustained. Trapped in the canal, it "kept scraping against [the walls], stripping the skin off its snout and even its pectoral fins", he told local media.

For now, South Korea is holding its breath, hoping Bukangi can find her way back to her home - the open sea.

CHANTHABURI, Thailand: Across a landscape where wild elephants roam and milky sap drips from endless rows of rubber trees, a decades-old tension is coming to a head.

Buddhist novices sweep dusty ground as smoke rises from small pits of burning rubbish. This is land that could soon be underwater.

Deep in the interior of eastern Thailand, a vast new reservoir could swallow thousands of hectares of forest, wildlife habitat and land occupied by rural communities.

The proposed Khlong Wang Tanot reservoir in Chanthaburi province – about four hours’ drive southeast of Bangkok – would store 99.5 million cubic metres of water, part of Thailand's efforts to secure supplies for its rapidly developing eastern seaboard.

But the project, first conceived in the 1990s, has become a flashpoint over the environmental and human costs of economic growth.

In August, Thailand’s Cabinet greenlit the project, with construction planned over seven years. But it cannot proceed without permission to use the protected forest in Khao Sip Ha Chan, including approval from the National Park Committee.

The government has said newer environmental evidence can still be considered before that process is completed.

Disagreement extends across government agencies, conservation groups and farming communities, with different views about whether the benefits of the reservoir outweigh the environmental and social costs.

The project has become a test of how Thailand balances its economic ambitions against commitments to protect forests, wildlife and communities living on the fringes of nature.

Among tin-roofed houses, flowering shrubs and coconut trees are those whose lives remain in flux.

The head of Ban Klong Yang village, Seeda Saengkla, lives inside the proposed reservoir area. Her community has 334 households and 654 residents. Many are prepared to accept the project but fear losing homes and livelihoods without sufficient compensation, she said.

“Naturally, leaving would be heartbreaking. But many villagers are willing to make that sacrifice,” she said.

“What they want is for the government and relevant agencies to help them survive after relocating. They don't want to move somewhere else only to end up in debt. They don't want to feel abandoned.”

Water shortages are not new to eastern Thailand. The Royal Irrigation Department (RID), a government agency, began studying the Wang Tanot watershed in the 1990s after serious dry-season shortages.

In the years since, those demands have only risen. Today, lucrative durian orchards increasingly spread across land once mostly planted with rubber, bringing with them a far greater thirst for water.

Thailand also faces growing uncertainty over rainfall, with more intense downpours and periods of drought creating challenges for water planning.

Pornpimol Thongbo, a durian farmer in Kaeng Hang Maeo district, said her family's water requirements have increased as their trees mature. During the dry season, they rely on ponds and groundwater, sometimes buying additional water when rainfall is insufficient.

Inadequate water can mean losing an entire harvest, she said. Still, she questioned the need for another reservoir when many local farmers still cannot access water from those already built.

Three reservoirs have already been constructed in the watershed. Wang Tanot would be the fourth and largest, completing a system designed to improve agricultural water security and reduce downstream flooding.

The reservoirs are fed by rainfall and surface runoff flowing through the watershed, capturing water during the wet season for use during drier months.

“Right now, we still haven't seen anything that would convince local residents that building the reservoir will actually benefit them,” Pornpimol said.

RID's Thammanoon Innooch, director of the Large-Scale Irrigation Construction Office in the province, projected the new reservoir would help serve the farmers and households that need water most.

That has energised other farmers in the area keen to see the project go ahead.

Thammanoon recognised that using protected forest land was a carefully considered issue and the project had been designed “to use only as much land within the national park as necessary”.

He acknowledged that up to 70 million cubic metres of surplus rainy-season runoff could eventually be transferred annually from the Wang Tanot watershed towards neighbouring Rayong province to support the wider EEC.

He said local water requirements would be met first and that the transfer volume would depend on rainfall and other conditions.

Industrial water requirements have risen substantially in the years since the reservoir was first conceived, with further expansion expected as Thailand courts a new generation of high-tech industries.

The weight of that need has locals questioning the fairness of the project, given what they say is a track record of being overlooked for water during dry periods.

“If it's for the benefit of the country, that's fine,” Pornpimol said. “But can local people benefit first? That's what matters to me.”

To drive along Thailand’s sprawling EEC area, which encompasses Chachoengsao, Chonburi and Rayong provinces, is to witness a landscape transformed.

Where fishing villages, rice fields and orchards once dominated, multilane expressways now cut through vast industrial estates, linking deep-sea ports, power stations and manufacturing plants.

In recent times, the data-centre boom has landed in the EEC too.

Three major facilities are already operating or expanding, while nine more are under construction, according to industry tracking data.

Government figures also show that overall, 29 data-centre projects have applied for investment incentives in the EEC, making up more than half of all such applications nationwide.

Thailand sees it as an opportunity to revive economic growth and attract investment and higher-skilled jobs as projects spill over from more constrained markets such as Singapore and Malaysia, said Kwankamol Chittrakul, a civil engineer and expert in water resources management.

For now, developers were taking advantage of a window of opportunity before Thailand introduced stricter environmental regulations for data centres, she said.

"It's a golden time. If you don't do it now, it will be more difficult in the future," she said.

The government is currently developing new regulations for data centres that would tighten oversight of their water and electricity consumption, environmental impacts and locations.

Still, hyperscale data centres add another major water consumer to a system already balancing agriculture, domestic demand and traditional industry.

Data centres require enormous computing power, generating heat that must be removed through cooling systems. Depending on the technology used, these facilities can consume substantial volumes of water.

Industry water demands in the EEC are expected to grow by 58 per cent between 2024 and 2037, according to Thailand's Office of National Water Resources.

The World Bank estimates that total annual water demand across the EEC's three provinces will reach 3.6 billion cubic metres by 2037.

And it warns that the region could face a water-supply shortfall of almost 40 per cent under average conditions, rising to 72 per cent in dry years, even with planned water infrastructure investments.

As supply lies in question, water availability is being actively used to convey confidence to would-be investors.

East Water, a major regional supplier, began supplying water to Bridge Data Centres in Chonburi in May, promoting its ability to provide uninterrupted supplies as a competitive advantage for Thailand's growing digital economy.

WHA Utilities and Power said this year that data centres can require 12 to 16 times as much water as conventional business customers. It is expanding water infrastructure and pursuing new contracts worth tens of millions of cubic metres annually to help cater to those requirements.

Industrial-estate developers are also expanding reservoirs, pipelines and water-treatment facilities to accommodate new customers.

Even the agency overseeing the EEC has acknowledged the growing challenge, with its next development plan placing greater emphasis on securing water and electricity for the region's expanding high-tech industries.

Wang Tanot is one of several water infrastructure projects intended to strengthen supplies across eastern Thailand, where industrial demand is expected to keep growing.

Somnuck Jongmeewasin, research director at civil society organisation EEC Watch, questioned why Thailand continued to focus on finding new supply rather than better managing existing water.

He said that greater industrial water recycling, more efficient irrigation and improved distribution could ease pressure on resources, while ensuring local communities receive their fair share before water is diverted to industrial users.

And he warned that the expansion of privately supplied industrial water could leave communities at a disadvantage as competition for resources intensifies.

“All data centres have money. They can buy electricity. They can buy water. But local people cannot buy,” he said. “So, this water war is not fair."

Alternatives to Wang Tanot have been proposed. The DNP has advocated a much smaller version of Wang Tanot that would flood only about 112 hectares of national park, combined with smaller reservoirs, greater storage at existing dams and more efficient water management.

Thammanoon said such measures could help, but would not provide enough water to meet projected demand.

The EEC Office is now working with the World Bank on expanding wastewater reuse, alongside measures to improve water efficiency and manage demand.

It also identified treated industrial wastewater reuse as an important way to reduce extraction from natural water sources and increase the resilience of EEC water supplies.

Back in Wang Tanot, the push for more water threatens another increasingly scarce resource: space for wild elephants.

Beyond Ban Klong Yang village, the landscape opens up to scattered tree-lined hillocks and long stretches of unbroken forest. It is cool and shaded with breaks in the undergrowth and well-trodden dirt paths used by families of elephants.

The national park and surrounds here form part of an important wildlife corridor, used by more than 100 elephants searching for food and water and moving to different areas depending on the season.

Part of Thailand's Eastern Forest Complex, it connects larger protected forests. But during drier periods, the elephants travel farther and increasingly enter agricultural areas to feed, said Supakarn Kaewchot, a senior veterinarian for the Department of National Parks, Wildlife and Plant Conservation (DNP).

“If the reservoir affects existing feeding routes, elephants will have to find new ways to reach food sources,” she said.

There is concern that flooding established feeding grounds and movement corridors could potentially bring them into closer contact with people, said Rattanawat Chaiyarat, an associate professor at the Faculty of Environment and Resource Studies at Mahidol University.

“The question is,” he said, “where will they go when the surrounding areas of the Eastern Forest Complex are entirely dedicated to agriculture and industry?”

Human-elephant conflict in the eastern region was “already quite severe” and it remained uncertain how well local communities could handle increased contact in future, he said.

Elephants rely on generational memory to navigate seasonal feeding grounds. Altering these routes disrupts herd cohesion and leadership, said Padet Siridumrong, a veterinarian who works with large animals and wildlife.

“When herd structures collapse, elephants scatter into small groups and enter agricultural areas, triggering recurring conflicts. This will lead to continuous spending on mitigation projects without resolving the root cause,” said Padet, the director of Nern Plub Wan Animal Hospital in Chonburi.

RID acknowledged habitat loss could increase the risk of elephants moving into agricultural areas and communities. But Thammanoon said the reservoir is necessary to meet long-term water demand and that RID has incorporated measures to monitor wildlife movements and maintain habitat connectivity.

Silchai Sae Jia, a 73-year-old rubber farmer, is already fed up with the large mammals.

Elephants regularly cross his plantation, pulling down trees and frightening workers. Some have stopped working there because of the danger, he said.

His land would remain above the reservoir's waterline, but he fears losing the surrounding elephant habitat could make his property uninhabitable.

“Some people might say I'm lucky. They might say I'll have a beautiful view of the reservoir, a million-baht view. But that's not how I see it. I think I won't be able to live here anymore,” he said.

Silchai said he understood Thailand's desire for growth. But he questioned who would ultimately pay the price for that ambition.

“Your development should not come at the expense of people like me,” he said. “You should not use our shoulders as a stepping stone towards your own prosperity.”
