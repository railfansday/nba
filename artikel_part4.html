Brazilians began voting in Sunday's (October 25, 2026) polarising presidential election between incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flavio Bolsonaro, a contest that will determine whether the U.S. will significantly expand its growing influence in Latin America.

Polls before Sunday's (October 25) vote showed the two candidates, who need more than 50% of the vote to become Brazil's next leader in January, in a neck-and-neck race. The election pits the left-of-centre veteran politician Mr. Lula against Mr. Bolsonaro, an ally of U.S. President Donald Trump and the son of imprisoned former President Jair Bolsonaro.

Flávio Bolsonaro bested Mr. Lula by more than 2.2 million votes in the first round of the election earlier this month. More than 33 million voters out of nearly 160 million did not turn out.

The difference between the two candidates is stark. Mr. Bolsonaro and Mr. Lula have opposing ideological views on issues ranging from U.S.-Brazil relations and climate change to social welfare.

A win for Mr. Bolsonaro would signify a pivot to the right by Latin America's most populous country and its largest economy in a year when Trump allies have notched victories in Chile, Peru and Colombia.

A victory for Mr. Lula would leave Brazil and Mexico, which is the region's second-most populous country and led by President Claudia Sheinbaum, with left-of-centre leaders.

In many ways, Brazil's runoff vote resembles a rematch between the country's two polarized political forces that trigger passion as much as loathing. Four years ago, Mr. Lula beat Mr. Bolsonaro's father by the narrowest margin since the country's return to democracy in 1985. Polls suggest that this election will be even closer.

Mr. Lula, who first governed the country between 2003 and 2010, has faced a tough reelection battle because of concerns about the cost of living and security. Flávio Bolsonaro made a tough-on-crime approach the cornerstone of his campaign and pledged to bring down food costs.

Both have been hit by corruption suspicions involving family members and allies, with Mr. Bolsonaro implicated in a sprawling scandal rocking Brazil. Mr. Lula spent more than a year in prison on corruption convictions that were eventually annulled.

Mr. Bolsonaro's admission that he accepted about $12 million from disgraced former banker Daniel Vorcaro to produce a film on his father's life has cast a shadow over his campaign. He is under investigation for corruption, money laundering and tax evasion in connection with the case.

In the lead-up to the vote, Mr. Lula frequently portrayed Mr. Bolsonaro, whose father is under house arrest for attempting a coup, as a threat to democracy. Mr. Bolsonaro has promised to seek an amnesty for his father if elected.

The next President will name at least three Supreme Court justices out of 11 in his next term due to upcoming retirements. Mr. Bolsonaro has long taken aim at the country's top court — which has been rocked by a corruption scandal — and promised to push for judiciary reform.

Mr. Lula, trying to lure undecided voters, has invested in costly incentives since he lost the first round of voting. His government's most recent moves have been endorsing a debt relief program for small businesses and promising free public transportation on weekends. A win for the incumbent came with the Senate's approval of the six-day working week — a popular proposal backed by the government.

Mr. Lula has criticised the U.S. President before Brazil's election, speaking about the risks of U.S. intervention in Brazilian elections after the Trump administration imposed high tariffs against Brazilian exports, sanctioned a Supreme Court justice who chaired the investigation that ended up resulting in the sentencing of Jair Bolsonaro and classified drug-trafficking groups as foreign terrorist organizations, which experts say may have been intended to make Lula look soft on crime.

Flávio Bolsonaro has touted his proximity to the White House and insisted that Brazil needs to strike better deals with the Trump administration, including the rare minerals economy. Brazil estimates that its deposits hold up to 21 million tons, second only to China. He denies he's being helped by the U.S. government.

Voting in Brazil is conducted electronically and the result is usually available within hours after polling stations close at 5 p.m. Brasilia time (2000 GMT).

#156. Is Donald Trump ready to do business with Myanmar?

A top US official recently met with Myanmar leader Min Aung Hlaing for the first time in years

Has Washington's long-standing policy of isolating the military regime in Myanmar now changed?

The revelation that a top official in the Trump administration met its leader Min Aung Hlaing last month, and the removal of some business figures from the US sanctions list, suggests that it has.

Until now, the US had had very little official contact with the regime following the 2021 coup, when Myanmar's military ousted democratically-elected leader Aung San Suu Kyi. More recently, it had applied a series of targeted sanctions against those judged to be supporting the junta.

The meeting on 15 and 16 September was with US Assistant Secretary of State for Intelligence and Research Michael Vance, and it took place in Myanmar's capital Nay Pyi Taw. Its ostensible focus was to secure the release of Adam Castillo, a US citizen who had been detained in June over a dispute with the local American Chamber of Commerce.

But Vance was the most senior US official to meet Min Aung Hlaing since he seized power, and they almost certainly discussed other matters of mutual interest.

Vance is also known to have met Burmese officials in Tokyo in June, again in Tokyo this month, and to have met representatives of the groups opposing military rule.

Signs of a shift in US policy towards Myanmar, though, go back to July last year, at the height of President Donald Trump's tariff war.

That was when Min Aung Hlaing, along with other leaders in the region, received a personal letter from the US president informing him that his country's shattered economy was about to be hit by, in Myanmar's case, 40% tariffs.

Other countries reeled in shock at the punitive levies imposed on their vital trade with a country they had once seen as a friend and ally.

Much of Myanmar has been devastated by a civil war that has been raging since 2021

He replied immediately to express his "sincere appreciation for this encouraging invitation to continue participating in the extraordinary Economy of the United States, the Number One Market in the World", mimicking Trump's hyperbolic phrasing and eccentric capitalisation.

"It is with great esteem that I wish to acknowledge Your Excellency's strong leadership in guiding your country toward national prosperity with the spirit of a true patriot."

This was not just a dictator's hunch that flattery might be the best way to win favours from Trump. Min Aung Hlaing's coup, and the civil war it ignited, had turned him into an international pariah.

His regime was largely unrecognised. He was accused of being a war criminal, with cases lodged against him at the International Criminal Court and the International Court of Justice. Aside from Russia and, more recently, China, he was shunned by other governments.

Yet here was the US president addressing him just like any other president or prime minister, as "Your Excellency. There was no mention in Trump's letter of the appalling human cost of Min Aung Hlaing's power seizure; of a democracy crushed and leader Aung San Suu Kyi jailed.

That same month the regime signed a $3m (Â£2.3m) contract with DCI, a long-standing Washington lobbying firm which had been used by a previous Burmese military regime in 2002 to try to improve its image.

Since then the military government has also hired Roger Stone, a controversial lobbyist and confidant of President Trump, at a fee of $50,000 a month.

Also around the same time Adam Castillo, a former US marine and Trump supporter who had just stepped down as president of the American Chamber of Commerce in Yangon, was in Washington trying to persuade Vice President JD Vance's team that it was time to re-engage.

Castillo believed that the US reliance on sanctions to isolate the junta had failed.

"Myanmar mattered to America not only as a morality play but as a strategic interest," he wrote in his book Finding Our Voice: A Story of Leadership in Crisis and the American Spirit Abroad. He discussed with the vice-president's team how the US "could engage Myanmar without war or isolation, loosen China's grip on critical minerals, and align peace with American interests".

The US dependence on rare earths from China had been exposed when China blocked them in retaliation against Trump's tariffs in April. The administration began prioritising finding alternative sources. Myanmar happens to have large reserves of rare earths which are currently being exploited solely for China's use.

At almost exactly the same time, the state department issued new guidance to US embassies around the world on what to say about elections. From now on, it said, "messages should avoid opining on the fairness or integrity of an electoral process, its legitimacy, or the democratic values of the country in question".

So, when Myanmar held its much-criticised general election in December and January, allowing Min Aung Hlaing to turn himself from coup leader into a notionally civilian president, the US did not, like most Western governments, condemn it as neither free nor fair.

For decades the US has consistently pronounced judgement on other countries' elections. Now the US had nothing to say.

Washington's decision to lift sanctions on some of those targeted by the Biden administration seemed only to confirm the new direction of US-Myanmar policy.

The tone is certainly different, but not so much the substance of US-Myanmar relations.

"In practice less has changed than the headlines suggest," says Richard Horsey, Myanmar adviser for the International Crisis Group, adding that Min Aung Hlaing is still sanctioned. "What is new is the level of contact and the location. Senior US officials have now been to Nay Pyi Taw, which is exactly what Min Aung Hlaing wanted. What he wants next is sanctions relief, and that will be harder."

It was another Trump initiative last year, though, which has arguably had a much greater impact on Myanmar: the dismantling of USAID, which slashed aid to opposition groups, dissident media and refugees by more than half.

There was also the decision, decried by human rights groups, to withdraw Temporary Protected Status from 4,000 Burmese who had sought sanctuary in the US. This has left them living in fear of being sent back, where many face being jailed, or worse.

As for the rare earths in Myanmar, few experts believe they will ever be of much use to the US. Most lie in areas contested by ethnic insurgent armies.

They are currently extracted by Chinese companies with long experience of operating in these lawless borderlands, and transported over the border to China for processing. It is difficult to see how the US could get access to them.

It is also doubtful that the US will get meaningful co-operation from the Myanmar government in combating the scam industry.

There have been a few highly-publicised actions against some of the larger scam compounds, but the syndicates running them have simply dispersed to smaller operations in more remote areas, and in any case most are run with the support of militia groups allied to the Myanmar military.

"Talking to Nay Pyi Taw is not wrong in itself," says Horsey.

"The question is what Washington asks for and what it gives away. It should use the channel for the things that matter most: humanitarian access, an end to airstrikes on civilians. It should keep talking to the opposition and the ethnic armed groups, who hold much of the country. And on scams, the most effective thing the US can do is to co-ordinate with other governments in the region, including China, to go after the money."

Perhaps the bigger question, though, is whether President Trump, distracted by domestic politics and the war on Iran, has the capacity to give any sustained attention to Myanmar, let alone enough to challenge the dominant influence of China.

#158. Commentary: Why work from home doesn’t mean work from anywhere

A Sydney-based worker sacked for secretly working from Singapore raises a question: Where exactly is “home” in work from home? Employers need to offer clarity, says Victor Seah of the Singapore University of Social Sciences.

A woman working with a laptop in the airport. (Photo: iStock/Julia Rios Gomez)

SINGAPORE: Someone I know routinely works from hotels overseas on his work-from-home days. His employer doesn’t know.

He isn’t an exception. Many more people quietly stretch “home” closer to where they live: a cafe, a co-working space or the waiting area outside their child’s enrichment class. Overseas is the far end of the same habit.

This was what a Sydney-based currency trader did when he worked from Singapore on a work-from-home day – except that his employer found out.

His manager had asked the company’s IT team to use its “FBI skills” to check on the trader’s whereabouts. The trader’s laptop was later traced to Singapore, and he was dismissed. It was not the trader’s first time working overseas without permission, based on an Oct 7 ruling by Australia’s Fair Work Commission. He had earlier worked from Bali while telling his manager he was at home waiting for a plumber.

The commission found that the trader had deliberately concealed his location and that his employer had a valid reason to dismiss him. But because he was not given a proper chance to respond, the dismissal was procedurally unfair. The trader was awarded a little more than four weeks’ pay.

The case stirred up discussion online, with some wondering how far can employees stretch the definition of “work from home” before crossing the line?

Work from home began as a business continuity measure and became the default during the COVID-19 pandemic. Today, it matters most to those juggling work and care.

In Singapore, the government has said flexible work arrangements are what caregivers find most useful. That places considerable weight on flexible work, and therefore on trust.

Our research at the Singapore University of Social Sciences with caregivers of persons with disabilities shows why. One caregiver had her flexible arrangement formally approved, yet felt resented by colleagues who still expected her previous level of commitment. She eventually left the workforce. Another, the mother of an adult son with autism, wants to return to work but doubts any employer would accept the short-notice interruptions his medical needs bring.

For both, trust from their employers and colleagues mattered more than having a formal flexible work arrangement.

Consider some familiar Singapore scenes: a parent clearing emails outside an enrichment class, a caregiver taking a call in a clinic waiting room while a loved one sees the doctor, someone working from a library, a cafe or their parents’ flat.

Most work-from-home policies never say whether these count. Many employees simply don't mention it, and many managers don't ask.

In Singapore, where the office is rarely far away, such arrangements are usually low-risk. The practical concerns are confidential calls in public, shared Wi-Fi and screens that others can see. Many of these situations also involve caregiving, precisely what flexible work is meant to support.

Working overseas without telling one’s employer is different. Beyond the dishonesty, it may expose the employer, not just the employee, to risks: licensing restrictions, as in the trader’s case, and possibly tax or immigration complications, cross-border data rules, insurance that may not apply abroad and time-zone mismatches.

Crucially, every hidden overseas work-from-home day makes employers more cautious with caregivers whose needs are real.

The obvious response is for employers to define work-from-home policies. But rules can feel like distrust.

In a well-known 2006 experiment, economists Armin Falk and Michael Kosfeld found that most participants worked less hard once they were controlled, and described it as a signal of distrust and a limit on their autonomy.

In 2023, a review of studies, involving 94 samples and more than 23,000 workers, found no evidence that electronic monitoring improves performance. Conversely,  electronic monitoring was associated with higher worker stress.

The “FBI skills” request from the trader’s manager illustrates the resulting loop: concealment invites surveillance, and surveillance signals distrust, which makes people less likely to be open.

Yet how rules are set makes a difference. The 2023 review found that more transparent, less invasive monitoring was linked to more positive attitudes. A separate UK study of knowledge professionals working remotely during the pandemic found that many accepted heavier workloads as a fair trade for the autonomy of working from home, especially where trust ran both ways.

The distinction is between clarity and control. Clarity sets out what is allowed and why. Control is about enforcement and that is where the costs to motivation tend to lie.

Employers can provide clarity without appearing controlling in several ways.

First, they can define work from home as permission, not suspicion. For example, by allowing employees to work anywhere in Singapore as long as they can work securely and reach the office when needed.

Second, explain the limits. If overseas work is restricted, say it is because of licensing, tax, visa or data concerns, not because the company doubts its staff.

Third, make asking easier than hiding. This can involve providing a quick approval route for working overseas or workcations.

Fourth, let individual teams set the details, such as when people should be reachable while working from home.

Fifth, resist adding new employee tracking, and rely on disclosure and results.

For employees, flexibility comes with a reciprocal obligation – being upfront about where they work.

For the parent at the enrichment centre or the caregiver at the clinic, working from home, outside the home, is often what flexible work was meant to make possible. What they need from employers is not closer watching, but clarity about what “home” means.

Victor Seah is Director of Behavioural Insights Centre of Excellence at the Singapore University of Social Sciences.

#159. Beyond bold statements: The evolving design philosophy of Singapore designer Colin Seah

From hotels with psychedelic gardens to serviced apartments designed to feel like home, Colin Seah's varied portfolio reflects a conviction that every space deserves its own identity.

For Colin Seah, founder of Ministry of Design, no two projects are quite alike. Each presents an opportunity to explore a different narrative and design approach. (Photos: Ministry of Design)

Mention designer Colin Seah and adjectives like “bold”, “irreverent” and “wacky” immediately come to mind to describe his projects.

Who can forget the New Majestic Hotel with its 30 individually designed rooms and funky pop art at every turn? Another is the all-white Canvas House for co-living concept Figment on Blair Road. More recently, The Standard Singapore stands out for its mid-century modern style and biophilic features, such as the terrarium integrated into the reception counter.

Which is why, when I was given a tour of his latest completed project, the renovated mixed-use Stamford Place, expectations were high that it would once again surprise. Instead, it felt like entering a home that was warm and cosy. Formerly known as Stamford Court, the L-shaped building is distinctive for its clock tower, dutifully keeping time at the junction of Stamford Road and Hill Street since 1996.

“I got older,” joked the 54-year-old, when asked what prompted this change.

Over the years, Seah has amassed considerable experience and evolved his approach through Ministry of Design, the firm he founded in 2004 and now runs with his wife, Joy Chan Seah.

“Our ethos has always been to question, disturb and then redefine. Back in the early days, we had an instinctively rebellious streak, finding things in projects which allowed us to fundamentally challenge and revolutionise our designs,” reflected Seah.

Nowadays, he is less inclined to do that. Instead, with maturity, he has become more interested in questioning the relevance of convention. “If there is no need for it, then we evolve or revolt,” he added.

The result is 66 units spread across two floors, done in a cream-coloured palette with an abundance of curves, such as the rounded corners of the built-in sofa and the pelmet below the recessed ceiling. For Seah, the priority was to make the spaces comfortable for long-stay guests.

“Three days in a very vibrant, vivacious hotel environment is fine, but maybe not a week or two. We are treading that fine line between having a narrative and sense of the guests feeling like themselves. They don’t have to adopt another persona to be here.”

Other gestures of “revolt” include APT’s corridor carpets, designed with a swirling geometric pattern inspired by time and horology. The rhythmic movement is slightly hypnotising – a stark contrast to the neo-noir lifts, whose saturated red glow evokes a moody, sultry vibe.

While the architecture was left untouched, Seah had a hand in updating the facade (now light grey), common spaces, new restaurant Stamford Social and co-working space The Great Room by Industrious. The building also houses seven tenanted spaces.

Conceptualising the project involved digesting the brief and coming up with a narrative, which Seah said is how he always starts. Fortunately for him, he had a “design ally” in the client, Ashish Manchharam, founder of Elevate Capital Group and a champion of restoring heritage buildings.

With Stamford Place, the vision was to retain the building’s character while refreshing the facade and clock tower. The interiors had to be laid-back and approachable, creating a hangout in the Civic District that also welcomes students from nearby educational institutions.

In response, Seah drew inspiration from the neighbouring colonial buildings in the Civic District, incorporating materials such as rattan and terracotta tiles. The windows have black frames, which he aptly called “mascara treatment”. Other horological references were incorporated too, such as a feature light on the reception ceiling.

The main entrance was also deliberately repositioned beneath the clock tower. “We wanted the project to be comfortable, inviting, iconic to a point, but not shouty,” he quipped.

Being commissioned to design Stamford Place soon after working on The Standard could give some creatives whiplash, but Seah was completely unfazed. In fact, he was pleased as punch.

“If you look at our recent in-progress and completed portfolio, nothing is alike. I think it is super encouraging, because it means that no one has pigeonholed us yet. We are the guys who can sink our teeth into a brief and give the client something that is comprehensive and holistic.”

At The Standard, the storytelling centred on merging the tropicality of the location with the irreverence of the brand. In response, Seah overlaid a timeless and contemporary feel onto its mid-century modern DNA. For instance, the terrarium-inspired reception counter depicts Singapore as a psychedelic garden. At Kaya restaurant, a botanical-themed wallpaper adds to the atmosphere.

Ascend to the rooms and a sense of quietness pervades, anchored by a timber-ribbed canopy running the width of the bed onto the ceiling. “There are subtle details, custom-designed furniture and an overall tranquillity with the abundance of natural light. The Standard was very good in supporting this balance of calmness as a contrast to the public areas,” said Seah.

A testament to his abilities came after the project was completed, when an Indonesian client who had experienced the property approached him to design a hotel in Long Beach, California. The brief: timeless, elegant, with an Asian touch. “You’d think that's so different from The Standard, but what he was saying is he recognised we can create these immersive worlds,” Seah explained.

He is committed to steering Ministry of Design in this direction, especially since he revels in being involved in every aspect of a project. His enthusiasm was palpable during the interview in APT’s Stamford Suite when he was asked how this approach manifests in his latest projects outside Singapore.

In Luoyang, an ancient imperial capital of China, he has designed The Reserve, part of Hilton’s Curio Collection. The mighty Yellow River inspired the concept, where the core narrative is that of the home of an art collector who has invited guests to stay. “There are moments where the culture is expressed in very vivid ways and other times, it forms a quieter backdrop,” he described.

Closer to home, Seah is keeping tabs on Divine Residences Kuala Lumpur, a 1,000-key hotel in Bukit Bintang that has started construction. Apart from the rooms, he was responsible for the design of all the other areas. These include the tunnel-like driveway, reception, restaurants and open-air rooftop bar on the 80th floor.

“The entrance is like a speakeasy, very moody and mysterious without the use of any clear glass. When you enter, the lighting and art make it feel lounge-like and sexy, like a forbidden space. We were tasked to do something quite differentiated from the surrounding five-star hotels and luxury residential towers.”

Having run Ministry of Design for 22 years, Seah surprised me when he named his team, rather than a completed work, as the project he is most excited about. “It's the project that’s never quite fully completed, but it's the most important one,” he said.

With a headcount of between 25 and 30, he and his wife have built a team aligned with their vision of designing immersive worlds. Like proud parents, both highlighted how deeply everyone cares about delivering quality work – a reflection of the Seahs’ belief in making the workplace an extension of themselves. “We are invested in treating people as equals, with respect,” he added.

The fruits of their labour were evident when they were able to take a three-week holiday to Denmark in August. With everyone looking out for one another, work-life balance is not just a corporate concept. “We had to work some but we had peace of mind while on the road, because we knew we had a good team manning the fort. That’s a very lovely thing, especially as you get older,” said Seah.

The rector of UMB University has publicly declared that 2,000 alumni will serve as agents of change in the national education sector. Records show this initiative is not merely about graduation numbers but a strategic push to reconstruct Indonesia's education paradigm in the era of artificial intelligence. What remains unclear is whether these alumni will possess the actual policy-making power to shift entrenched institutional norms or remain symbolic figures. Sources confirm the program was launched amid declining graduate employability rates, with the rector arguing that traditional curricula no longer meet the demands of a digital economy. The bold claim that 2,000 agents can reshape a system serving 50 million students raises urgent questions about scalability and accountability.

The rector’s move to reposition alumni as change agents signals a fundamental shift in how higher education institutions view their post-graduation responsibility. #168. Not every maritime threat needs a warship: Why Singapore is turning to offshore patrol vessels

From maritime terrorism to drones, navies face a wider range of threats that do not always fit neatly into either law enforcement or conventional warfare, say analysts.

The Republic of Singapore Navy's new Sentinel-class offshore patrol vessel launched in Berne, Germany on Oct 1, 2026. (Photo: MINDEF)

SINGAPORE: Deploying heavily armed, grey-hulled warships to tackle every maritime security threat along important sea lanes is akin to taking a hammer to every problem, defence experts said, adding that increasingly complex challenges call for more measured responses at sea.

Earlier this month, the Republic of Singapore Navy (RSN) launched its first Sentinel-class offshore patrol vessel, christened Sentinel. It is the first of four government vessels built to meet a broader spectrum of maritime security operations.

Their calibrated capabilities will allow Singapore to respond to incidents in a measured and proportionate manner, giving the government "more options" at sea, the Ministry of Defence (MINDEF) said during the vessel’s launch.

They offer an alternative to conventional warships, said Mr Nicholas Lim, senior fellow and coordinator of defence and military relations at the S Rajaratnam School of International Studies (RSIS) in Nanyang Technological University.

Unlike the standard grey hull on most combatant and auxiliary surface ships that keeps them camouflaged against the marine backdrop, the offshore patrol vessels sport white hulls.

"You cannot deploy your grey-hulled warships for everything. It's like you're using a hammer for every single problem," said Mr Lim.

Regional countries have made similar moves. In 2024, Malaysia's Maritime Enforcement Agency took delivery of a new offshore patrol vessel while the Indonesian navy launched two such ships.

Unlike the heavily militarised environment in the Middle East, the sea lanes around Singapore face a different breed of risk, experts said.

From maritime terrorism and trafficking to drones and threats to undersea infrastructure, modern navies in the region must manage risks that sit in the ambiguous grey zone between routine law enforcement and conventional warfare.

More than just dealing with piracy and sea robbery, navies need to respond to different types of security challenges, said Mr Lim.

Recent tensions across vital global choke points like the Strait of Hormuz demonstrated the urgent demand for vessels that can deter threats and defuse flashpoints before they disrupt international trade.

Like the Strait of Hormuz, the Straits of Malacca and Singapore are among the world's most important shipping routes, carrying nearly a quarter of global seaborne trade and almost a third of global oil and gas supplies.

But their geopolitical and security environments are worlds apart, experts stressed.

Maritime security in the Strait of Hormuz is closely intertwined with broader military and geopolitical tensions, said associate research fellow from the RSIS' maritime security programme Chong De Xian.

"In contrast, the Straits of Malacca and Singapore operate in a relatively stable security environment, where the littoral states share a strong interest in keeping the waterways open and secure," he added, referring to Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore.

Beyond these threats, geopolitical tensions and armed conflict have exposed the vulnerability of international shipping routes.

The Strait of Hormuz carried about one-fifth of the world's energy shipments before the war between the United States and Iran began earlier this year.

On Oct 14, two Singapore-registered container vessels were struck by unknown projectiles while transiting the strait. One sank after sustaining damage and catching fire.

The narrow waterway at the southern tip of the Red Sea, which serves as a gateway to the Suez Canal from the Indian Ocean, is a key shipping route and an alternative for the world's largest oil exporter Saudi Arabia to export crude oil.

Such developments show how threats to commercial shipping can disrupt international trade and energy flows even without physically closing maritime chokepoints, Mr Chong said.

"The two situations are not comparable," said Dr Storey.

"The most important difference between the two is that in Southeast Asia the littoral states are committed to keeping the Straits of Malacca and Singapore free, open and safe for international shipping while Iran has weaponised the Strait of Hormuz."

Mr Lim similarly pointed to the strong cooperation among Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia, which share an interest in keeping the waterways open.

He said the situation in the Strait of Hormuz nevertheless offered an important lesson on the need to uphold international law, particularly the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), a set of rules that governs the world's oceans.

It allocates rights and legal authority to states, and establishes regulations for the peaceful use of the sea and its resources.

"The one big lesson learnt from the Strait of Hormuz is that we cannot take it for granted that access is always there. We have to protect this UNCLOS to ensure that the key international straits are open," he said.

But he was confident that a similar situation would not unfold in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, given the differences in geography, history, culture and shared interests.

Today, regional threats at sea include illegal fishing, smuggling and maritime terrorism, said Mr Lim, who served in the RSN for about 30 years before retiring as a colonel last year.

Fishing vessels, for instance, can be exploited to transport drugs or be used for smuggling, while maritime terrorism could involve the hijacking of liquefied natural gas (LNG) carriers and turning them into potential "floating bombs".

While piracy and armed robbery incidents remain ever-present concerns in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, Mr Lim said the numbers fluctuate based on enforcement drives and weather patterns.

"We do not have any serious kind of piracy or sea robbery incidents. By serious I mean (the) hijacking of entire ships. We have not seen that, fingers crossed, for many, many years," he said, crediting the coordinated efforts of Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia.

Dr Ian Storey, principal fellow at the ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute, agreed that piracy and sea robbery have been effectively managed, with closer cooperation among the three countries' navies and law enforcement agencies significantly reducing maritime crime over the past two decades.

Nonetheless, Southeast Asia's wider maritime security landscape presents a "mixed picture", he said.

Transnational trafficking in narcotics, weapons as well as people still persist, alongside longstanding territorial disputes and overlapping maritime boundaries that continue to generate tensions, particularly in the South China Sea.

Technological shifts can also expose new vulnerabilities, said experts.

The proliferation of aerial and underwater drones poses an increasing threat to the region, while critical infrastructure, including undersea cables and energy pipelines, remains vulnerable to both accidents and sabotage, Dr Storey said.

Because undersea assets are spread across vast seabed tracts, protecting them from deliberate foul play is difficult.

What complicates efforts are the geographical challenges and evidential difficulties in proving deliberate interference, said RSIS’ Mr Chong.

Such disruptions to critical maritime infrastructure can have far-reaching economic and security consequences, he said.

Illicit maritime activities have also become more sophisticated, with deceptive shipping practices, sanctions evasion and shadow fleets making surveillance and enforcement harder, he added.

Singapore's location alongside one of the world's busiest shipping routes, and its reliance on maritime trade and essential supplies, have always presented particular challenges for the RSN, said Mr Chong, himself a former RSN officer.

"Such situations require responses that are appropriate and proportionate to the circumstances. Some incidents may be resolved through warnings and other less-lethal measures, while others may require more forceful intervention."

"This is particularly important in Singapore’s congested maritime environment where incidents must be managed without unnecessarily disrupting commercial shipping or escalating tensions."

There can also be significant political and diplomatic implications, especially when foreign vessels are involved.

"Dedicated maritime security vessels with a less militaristic posture compared to conventional warships further support this approach by allowing Singapore to protect its maritime interests while minimising the risk of escalation," Mr Chong said.

The growing complexity of maritime security threats has also placed greater demands on navies to manage incidents that fall between routine law enforcement and conventional naval warfare, he added.

White hulls and red racing stripes are in line with vessels operated by maritime security agencies around the world, Mindef previously said.

Unlike the grey hulls traditionally associated with heavily armed warships, white-hulled vessels are seen as less threatening or provocative, Dr Storey said.

Mr Lim similarly noted that conventional warships with their grey hulls carry a "certain signature" that may be unnecessary for some maritime security operations.

Raising an example of a sea robbery, he said there is no need to deploy a warship to deal with a pirate boat.

"Can't you just use, for example, an offshore patrol vessel that's big enough, that has the manpower and the equipment to deal with it? Why do you need to send an advanced warship with missiles to deal with a pirate boat?"

Singapore's new offshore patrol vessel will “inherit” the maritime security missions of the existing Sentinel-class Maritime Security and Response Vessels (MSRVs) while also safeguarding the country's territorial waters, said Mindef when the vessels were launched.

The MSRVs were also touted to provide more calibrated options to respond to maritime incidents. While they also have grey-coloured hulls, the MSRVs all bear a red “racing” stripe on their bow.

While these responsibilities remain largely unchanged, the new offshore patrol vessels offer enhanced capabilities and a wider range of options to take on these missions, Mr Chong said.

For instance, responding to a suspicious or non-compliant vessel may require first establishing its identity and intentions, issuing warnings and intercepting it before more forceful intervention becomes necessary.

The offshore patrol vessel can deploy smaller craft for closer investigation or intervention as situations develop.

They are also equipped to address emerging threats. Their weapons suite features the STRALES guided gun, which enables the vessels to counter unmanned threats, Mr Chong noted.

The vessels could also support other agencies in responding to suspicious activities near sensitive maritime installations

Their greater endurance would allow them to maintain a more sustained presence at sea.

The offshore patrol vessels have more than five times the displacement of the existing MSRVs and are designed for longer operations.

Each of the new vessels has a displacement of 2,700 tonnes, measures 94.85m long and 15.2m wide, and carries a baseline crew of about 40.

In comparison, the MSRVs have a displacement of 525 tonnes, measure 55m long and 8.6m wide, and carry a baseline crew of about 30.

The existing vessels have an endurance of 1,800 nautical miles, compared with more than 4,000 nautical miles for the new vessels, allowing the latter to travel farther without refuelling or resupply.

But their increased size and endurance do not necessarily signal an expansion of Singapore's maritime security operations farther from home, experts said.

Instead, they offer the RSN greater flexibility to remain at sea longer and for extended periods.

"While their enhanced capabilities provide the option of operating farther offshore when required, they are equally valuable for maintaining a more persistent maritime security presence closer to home," Mr Chong said.

Ultimately, the new vessels reflect the need for maritime security forces to adapt to grey-zone  threats, ensuring that Singapore, as well as its regional partners, can protect vital waterways without reaching for a sledgehammer.

#172. Commentary: Why work from home doesn’t mean work from anywhere

A Sydney-based worker sacked for secretly working from Singapore raises a question: Where exactly is “home” in work from home? Employers need to offer clarity, says Victor Seah of the Singapore University of Social Sciences.

A woman working with a laptop in the airport. (Photo: iStock/Julia Rios Gomez)

SINGAPORE: Someone I know routinely works from hotels overseas on his work-from-home days. His employer doesn’t know.

He isn’t an exception. Many more people quietly stretch “home” closer to where they live: a cafe, a co-working space or the waiting area outside their child’s enrichment class. Overseas is the far end of the same habit.

This was what a Sydney-based currency trader did when he worked from Singapore on a work-from-home day – except that his employer found out.

His manager had asked the company’s IT team to use its “FBI skills” to check on the trader’s whereabouts. The trader’s laptop was later traced to Singapore, and he was dismissed. It was not the trader’s first time working overseas without permission, based on an Oct 7 ruling by Australia’s Fair Work Commission. He had earlier worked from Bali while telling his manager he was at home waiting for a plumber.

The commission found that the trader had deliberately concealed his location and that his employer had a valid reason to dismiss him. But because he was not given a proper chance to respond, the dismissal was procedurally unfair. The trader was awarded a little more than four weeks’ pay.

The case stirred up discussion online, with some wondering how far can employees stretch the definition of “work from home” before crossing the line?

Work from home began as a business continuity measure and became the default during the COVID-19 pandemic. Today, it matters most to those juggling work and care.

In Singapore, the government has said flexible work arrangements are what caregivers find most useful. That places considerable weight on flexible work, and therefore on trust.

Our research at the Singapore University of Social Sciences with caregivers of persons with disabilities shows why. One caregiver had her flexible arrangement formally approved, yet felt resented by colleagues who still expected her previous level of commitment. She eventually left the workforce. Another, the mother of an adult son with autism, wants to return to work but doubts any employer would accept the short-notice interruptions his medical needs bring.

For both, trust from their employers and colleagues mattered more than having a formal flexible work arrangement.

Consider some familiar Singapore scenes: a parent clearing emails outside an enrichment class, a caregiver taking a call in a clinic waiting room while a loved one sees the doctor, someone working from a library, a cafe or their parents’ flat.

Most work-from-home policies never say whether these count. Many employees simply don't mention it, and many managers don't ask.

In Singapore, where the office is rarely far away, such arrangements are usually low-risk. The practical concerns are confidential calls in public, shared Wi-Fi and screens that others can see. Many of these situations also involve caregiving, precisely what flexible work is meant to support.

Working overseas without telling one’s employer is different. Beyond the dishonesty, it may expose the employer, not just the employee, to risks: licensing restrictions, as in the trader’s case, and possibly tax or immigration complications, cross-border data rules, insurance that may not apply abroad and time-zone mismatches.

Crucially, every hidden overseas work-from-home day makes employers more cautious with caregivers whose needs are real.

The obvious response is for employers to define work-from-home policies. But rules can feel like distrust.

In a well-known 2006 experiment, economists Armin Falk and Michael Kosfeld found that most participants worked less hard once they were controlled, and described it as a signal of distrust and a limit on their autonomy.

In 2023, a review of studies, involving 94 samples and more than 23,000 workers, found no evidence that electronic monitoring improves performance. Conversely,  electronic monitoring was associated with higher worker stress.

The “FBI skills” request from the trader’s manager illustrates the resulting loop: concealment invites surveillance, and surveillance signals distrust, which makes people less likely to be open.

Yet how rules are set makes a difference. The 2023 review found that more transparent, less invasive monitoring was linked to more positive attitudes. A separate UK study of knowledge professionals working remotely during the pandemic found that many accepted heavier workloads as a fair trade for the autonomy of working from home, especially where trust ran both ways.

The distinction is between clarity and control. Clarity sets out what is allowed and why. Control is about enforcement and that is where the costs to motivation tend to lie.

Employers can provide clarity without appearing controlling in several ways.

First, they can define work from home as permission, not suspicion. For example, by allowing employees to work anywhere in Singapore as long as they can work securely and reach the office when needed.

Second, explain the limits. If overseas work is restricted, say it is because of licensing, tax, visa or data concerns, not because the company doubts its staff.

Third, make asking easier than hiding. This can involve providing a quick approval route for working overseas or workcations.

Fourth, let individual teams set the details, such as when people should be reachable while working from home.

Fifth, resist adding new employee tracking, and rely on disclosure and results.

For employees, flexibility comes with a reciprocal obligation – being upfront about where they work.

For the parent at the enrichment centre or the caregiver at the clinic, working from home, outside the home, is often what flexible work was meant to make possible. What they need from employers is not closer watching, but clarity about what “home” means.

Victor Seah is Director of Behavioural Insights Centre of Excellence at the Singapore University of Social Sciences.

#184. Not every maritime threat needs a warship: Why Singapore is turning to offshore patrol vessels

From maritime terrorism to drones, navies face a wider range of threats that do not always fit neatly into either law enforcement or conventional warfare, say analysts.

The Republic of Singapore Navy's new Sentinel-class offshore patrol vessel launched in Berne, Germany on Oct 1, 2026. (Photo: MINDEF)

SINGAPORE: Deploying heavily armed, grey-hulled warships to tackle every maritime security threat along important sea lanes is akin to taking a hammer to every problem, defence experts said, adding that increasingly complex challenges call for more measured responses at sea.

Earlier this month, the Republic of Singapore Navy (RSN) launched its first Sentinel-class offshore patrol vessel, christened Sentinel. It is the first of four government vessels built to meet a broader spectrum of maritime security operations.

Their calibrated capabilities will allow Singapore to respond to incidents in a measured and proportionate manner, giving the government "more options" at sea, the Ministry of Defence (MINDEF) said during the vessel’s launch.

They offer an alternative to conventional warships, said Mr Nicholas Lim, senior fellow and coordinator of defence and military relations at the S Rajaratnam School of International Studies (RSIS) in Nanyang Technological University.

Unlike the standard grey hull on most combatant and auxiliary surface ships that keeps them camouflaged against the marine backdrop, the offshore patrol vessels sport white hulls.

"You cannot deploy your grey-hulled warships for everything. It's like you're using a hammer for every single problem," said Mr Lim.

Regional countries have made similar moves. In 2024, Malaysia's Maritime Enforcement Agency took delivery of a new offshore patrol vessel while the Indonesian navy launched two such ships.

Unlike the heavily militarised environment in the Middle East, the sea lanes around Singapore face a different breed of risk, experts said.

From maritime terrorism and trafficking to drones and threats to undersea infrastructure, modern navies in the region must manage risks that sit in the ambiguous grey zone between routine law enforcement and conventional warfare.

More than just dealing with piracy and sea robbery, navies need to respond to different types of security challenges, said Mr Lim.

Recent tensions across vital global choke points like the Strait of Hormuz demonstrated the urgent demand for vessels that can deter threats and defuse flashpoints before they disrupt international trade.

Like the Strait of Hormuz, the Straits of Malacca and Singapore are among the world's most important shipping routes, carrying nearly a quarter of global seaborne trade and almost a third of global oil and gas supplies.

But their geopolitical and security environments are worlds apart, experts stressed.

Maritime security in the Strait of Hormuz is closely intertwined with broader military and geopolitical tensions, said associate research fellow from the RSIS' maritime security programme Chong De Xian.

"In contrast, the Straits of Malacca and Singapore operate in a relatively stable security environment, where the littoral states share a strong interest in keeping the waterways open and secure," he added, referring to Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore.

Beyond these threats, geopolitical tensions and armed conflict have exposed the vulnerability of international shipping routes.

The Strait of Hormuz carried about one-fifth of the world's energy shipments before the war between the United States and Iran began earlier this year.

The narrow waterway at the southern tip of the Red Sea, which serves as a gateway to the Suez Canal from the Indian Ocean, is a key shipping route and an alternative for the world's largest oil exporter Saudi Arabia to export crude oil.

Such developments show how threats to commercial shipping can disrupt international trade and energy flows even without physically closing maritime chokepoints, Mr Chong said.

"The two situations are not comparable," said Dr Storey.

"The most important difference between the two is that in Southeast Asia the littoral states are committed to keeping the Straits of Malacca and Singapore free, open and safe for international shipping while Iran has weaponised the Strait of Hormuz."

Mr Lim similarly pointed to the strong cooperation among Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia, which share an interest in keeping the waterways open.

He said the situation in the Strait of Hormuz nevertheless offered an important lesson on the need to uphold international law, particularly the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), a set of rules that governs the world's oceans.

It allocates rights and legal authority to states, and establishes regulations for the peaceful use of the sea and its resources.

"The one big lesson learnt from the Strait of Hormuz is that we cannot take it for granted that access is always there. We have to protect this UNCLOS to ensure that the key international straits are open," he said.

But he was confident that a similar situation would not unfold in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, given the differences in geography, history, culture and shared interests.

Today, regional threats at sea include illegal fishing, smuggling and maritime terrorism, said Mr Lim, who served in the RSN for about 30 years before retiring as a colonel last year.

Fishing vessels, for instance, can be exploited to transport drugs or be used for smuggling, while maritime terrorism could involve the hijacking of liquefied natural gas (LNG) carriers and turning them into potential "floating bombs".

While piracy and armed robbery incidents remain ever-present concerns in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore, Mr Lim said the numbers fluctuate based on enforcement drives and weather patterns.

"We do not have any serious kind of piracy or sea robbery incidents. By serious I mean (the) hijacking of entire ships. We have not seen that, fingers crossed, for many, many years," he said, crediting the coordinated efforts of Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia.

Dr Ian Storey, principal fellow at the ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute, agreed that piracy and sea robbery have been effectively managed, with closer cooperation among the three countries' navies and law enforcement agencies significantly reducing maritime crime over the past two decades.

Nonetheless, Southeast Asia's wider maritime security landscape presents a "mixed picture", he said.

Transnational trafficking in narcotics, weapons as well as people still persist, alongside longstanding territorial disputes and overlapping maritime boundaries that continue to generate tensions, particularly in the South China Sea.

Technological shifts can also expose new vulnerabilities, said experts.

The proliferation of aerial and underwater drones poses an increasing threat to the region, while critical infrastructure, including undersea cables and energy pipelines, remains vulnerable to both accidents and sabotage, Dr Storey said.

Because undersea assets are spread across vast seabed tracts, protecting them from deliberate foul play is difficult.

What complicates efforts are the geographical challenges and evidential difficulties in proving deliberate interference, said RSIS’ Mr Chong.

Such disruptions to critical maritime infrastructure can have far-reaching economic and security consequences, he said.

Illicit maritime activities have also become more sophisticated, with deceptive shipping practices, sanctions evasion and shadow fleets making surveillance and enforcement harder, he added.

Singapore's location alongside one of the world's busiest shipping routes, and its reliance on maritime trade and essential supplies, have always presented particular challenges for the RSN, said Mr Chong, himself a former RSN officer.

"Such situations require responses that are appropriate and proportionate to the circumstances. Some incidents may be resolved through warnings and other less-lethal measures, while others may require more forceful intervention."

Having a wider range of capabilities allows maritime security forces to take graduated responses as situations develop, without prematurely resorting to more forceful options, he said.

"This is particularly important in Singapore’s congested maritime environment where incidents must be managed without unnecessarily disrupting commercial shipping or escalating tensions."

There can also be significant political and diplomatic implications, especially when foreign vessels are involved.

"Dedicated maritime security vessels with a less militaristic posture compared to conventional warships further support this approach by allowing Singapore to protect its maritime interests while minimising the risk of escalation," Mr Chong said.

The growing complexity of maritime security threats has also placed greater demands on navies to manage incidents that fall between routine law enforcement and conventional naval warfare, he added.

Unlike the grey hulls traditionally associated with heavily armed warships, white-hulled vessels are seen as less threatening or provocative, Dr Storey said.

Mr Lim similarly noted that conventional warships with their grey hulls carry a "certain signature" that may be unnecessary for some maritime security operations.

Raising an example of a sea robbery, he said there is no need to deploy a warship to deal with a pirate boat.

"Can't you just use, for example, an offshore patrol vessel that's big enough, that has the manpower and the equipment to deal with it? Why do you need to send an advanced warship with missiles to deal with a pirate boat?"

Singapore's new offshore patrol vessel will “inherit” the maritime security missions of the existing Sentinel-class Maritime Security and Response Vessels (MSRVs) while also safeguarding the country's territorial waters, said Mindef when the vessels were launched.

The MSRVs were also touted to provide more calibrated options to respond to maritime incidents. While they also have grey-coloured hulls, the MSRVs all bear a red “racing” stripe on their bow.

While these responsibilities remain largely unchanged, the new offshore patrol vessels offer enhanced capabilities and a wider range of options to take on these missions, Mr Chong said.

For instance, responding to a suspicious or non-compliant vessel may require first establishing its identity and intentions, issuing warnings and intercepting it before more forceful intervention becomes necessary.

The offshore patrol vessel can deploy smaller craft for closer investigation or intervention as situations develop.

They are also equipped to address emerging threats. Their weapons suite features the STRALES guided gun, which enables the vessels to counter unmanned threats, Mr Chong noted.

The vessels could also support other agencies in responding to suspicious activities near sensitive maritime installations

Their greater endurance would allow them to maintain a more sustained presence at sea.

The offshore patrol vessels have more than five times the displacement of the existing MSRVs and are designed for longer operations.

Each of the new vessels has a displacement of 2,700 tonnes, measures 94.85m long and 15.2m wide, and carries a baseline crew of about 40.

In comparison, the MSRVs have a displacement of 525 tonnes, measure 55m long and 8.6m wide, and carry a baseline crew of about 30.

The existing vessels have an endurance of 1,800 nautical miles, compared with more than 4,000 nautical miles for the new vessels, allowing the latter to travel farther without refuelling or resupply.

But their increased size and endurance do not necessarily signal an expansion of Singapore's maritime security operations farther from home, experts said.

Instead, they offer the RSN greater flexibility to remain at sea longer and for extended periods.

"While their enhanced capabilities provide the option of operating farther offshore when required, they are equally valuable for maintaining a more persistent maritime security presence closer to home," Mr Chong said.

Ultimately, the new vessels reflect the need for maritime security forces to adapt to grey-zone  threats, ensuring that Singapore, as well as its regional partners, can protect vital waterways without reaching for a sledgehammer.
