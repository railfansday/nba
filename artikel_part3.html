#101. Want to get ahead at work? This behavioural psychologist turns career challenges into games

From discovering your strengths to creating opportunities, networking and navigating workplace politics, Ritika Datta turns career challenges into games. She tells CNA Women how female-led communities helped her get her start in Singapore’s diversity, equity and inclusion space.

Ritika Datta is a behavioural psychologist who creates games for learning in Singapore’s diversity, equity and inclusion space. (Photo: Felly Loi)

When Ritika Datta founded Hakopi in 2023 to create diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) games, she reached out to female-led communities to find out if they wanted to feature her games at their events.

The behavioural psychologist had just moved to Singapore with her husband. Having previously built games for adult learners, she wanted to continue her work.

Her first event was co-organised by Moxy, a platform that drives structural change for women in the workplace, and Lean In Network, Singapore, a non-profit that connects women and empowers them to reach their career goals.

“It spiralled from there – in a good way,” she said. Of the 200 women in attendance, many were interested in bringing her to their organisations.

“The women’s community was just so much easier for me to get into, even from a networking perspective, because they were very warm and open to having conversations and trying out new things,” the 34-year-old told CNA Women.

The first two game sessions she ran were, coincidentally, women-centric. This became a “niche” for Hakopi and her portfolio grew from there.

Datta’s games come in two buckets. The first are “games that change the world”. These aim to change the culture and how people work, tackling issues like belonging and allyship. The second are games to help you thrive in today’s world, teaching skills such as networking or getting more visibility in your career.

“If you have to wait for the world to become a better place before you do anything, you’re going to be waiting forever, so it has to work hand in hand,” she said.

One of the first games Datta created was Serendipity, about creating and grabbing opportunities.

“We believe – especially in the Asian context – that if you put your head down and work really hard, good things will come to you,” Datta said. “But as you move forward in life, you’ll realise that’s not quite true.”

She added: “We talk about this concept of what I call the surface area of serendipity. There are opportunities all around you, so if you’re sitting at your desk, you only have access to the opportunities around your desk.

“But the more you put yourself out there in meaningful ways, the more your surface area of serendipity increases and the more likely these opportunities are to find you.”

Players play in teams and step into the shoes of a particular character, making decisions for them to expand their surface area of serendipity.

Another game Datta created is Power Play, which gets players to recognise that workplace politics is not a dirty concept but a skill they can use to influence others. They play a product manager who needs to navigate internal politics and influence key stakeholders to get an idea approved.

“This game has a lot more debate and it’s fun because it’s so deeply rooted in us that I shouldn’t have to do all these extra things to get my work recognised,” said Datta.

Then there is Strength To Strength, a detective-themed game where players uncover their own strengths and learn how to communicate them with confidence.

“It gives people the tools to discover their own strengths and also realise how to fight back the inner voices – or sometimes also the external voices – that are telling them that’s not something you’re good at,” said Datta.

Although she collaborates often with female-focused communities, such as Moxy, The Elevate Group, Lean In Network, Singapore and Young Women’s Leadership Connection, most of her 15 flagship games can be played by both men and women.

Clients, which have included Google, LVMH, Macquarie Group and Marriott International, can take existing games or ask to modify stories or language to fit the company or industry.

Some may commission her to build a game that focuses on a particular issue – for example, she’s building one on male allyship.

Datta describes the process of developing a game as “a bit of a structured science”. She starts by looking at the learning needs of the topic through research and discussions with the target audience.

She takes inspiration from commercial board games when deciding on mechanics, such as selecting different cards or moving through different zones on a board.

“Once the mechanics are in place, I build the overarching story because you want players to feel like this is something they can relate to,” she explained.

It takes the Indian national around a month to create a new game. She runs Hakopi by herself but sometimes gets help with visual design work.

In September 2025, Datta conducted an introduction to finance workshop for about 200 migrant labourers.

The construction workers learnt about the relational aspects of budgeting, such as how to have a conversation with someone from home asking for money, how to tell them how much they can send and what they have to keep for themselves.

“Language was a problem as we had five different nationalities, with minimal English,” she recalled. “That was an interesting challenge, to make the language simple but teach complex concepts they could relate to.

“We also had to figure out how to run a game at a construction site during their lunch break. Luckily, they had a good time because it was different, counting fake money and throwing game chips at each other,” she added.

Datta said she hasn’t come across many others doing corporate workshops in the gender game space.

“The idea of using games for learning is fairly new, so you want to make sure that everyone in this space succeeds so that eventually you will succeed too.

“It’s a great community that has enough space for all of us to not feel like we’re stepping on each other’s toes,” she said.

Datta hopes that participants remember what they’ve taken away from her games.

“I don’t want them to comment on how the character was so well written or that the game design was amazing,” she explained. “I want them to say, ‘I didn’t realise that I was doing thing A when I should be doing thing B.’ That’s my internal measure of success."

She also wants to help people realise they are empowered to make changes, and that change doesn’t have to be a massive thing that’s scary and unattainable – it can be small steps.

“I want the games to show people that you have the power to change,” Datta said. “Even changing a small behaviour will have a cascading effect.”

#103. Nvidia US$2.000 Lisensi GPU: Hambatan Inovasi AI di Indonesia

Nvidia menerbitkan lisensi software AI seharga US$2.000 per GPU per tahun, menciptakan hambatan finansial besar bagi startup Indonesia. Biaya ini setara dengan gaji engineer junior dan modal awal usaha mikro, memaksa pengembang untuk berpindah ke...

Saat pasar global berdetak kencang karena kebijakan baru Nvidia, Indonesia kini menghadapi pilihan strategis yang tak bisa diabaikan. Kementerian Ekonomi Kreatif baru saja mengumumkan pembangunan Koridor Kreatif Berbasis AI antara Indonesia dan India di KEK Singhasari, sekaligus proyek infrastruktur NUSA sempat memicu perdebatan tentang adoptasi teknologi. Namun di balik pengumuman progres, muncul ancaman konkret: Nvidia melarang lisensi software AI seharga US$2.000 per GPU per tahun. Kebijakan ini bukan sekadar birokrasi, melainkan titik balik yang akan menentukan apakah sektor kreatif dan industri di Indonesia bisa memanfaatkan AI atau justru terkunci di luar jangkauan. Kebijakan ini akan membebani anggaran pengembang lokal dan menghambat eksportasi solusi AI Indonesia ke pasar internasional, sekaligus memaksa kita untuk bertanya sejauh mana kita siap mengandalkan teknologi asing dengan harga yang semakin mahal.

Dampak Finansial pada Startup dan Pengembang Lokal

Nvidia mendorong harga lisensi software AI ke atas US$2.000 per GPU per tahun. Untuk startup Indonesia yang operasi dengan margin tipis, biaya ini setara dengan gaji tahunan seorang engineer junior di kota besar. Sebuah tim pengembangan AI yang membutuhkan 5 GPU untuk training model akan menghabiskan sekitar US$10.000 per tahun hanya untuk lisensi, jumlah yang setara dengan modal awal banyak usaha mikro di sektor teknologi. Kebijakan ini secara langsung mempengaruhi proyek seperti PRAFASA dari Bondan Prakoso & Fade2Black, di mana AI digunakan untuk menciptakan film musik, namun sekarang menghadangi batu belok biaya operasional yang tak terduga. Banyak pengembang lokal akan terpaksa berpindah ke model open-source, meskipun kualitas dan dukungan teknisnya masih kalah lengket dari ekosistem proprietary Nvidia. Transisi ke open-source memang menjanjikan fleksibilitas, namun bagi proyek yang bergantung pada optimisasi hardware terbaru dan driver stabil, perubahan ini memerlukan waktu refactoring yang signifikan dan risiko penurunan performa yang tidak bisa diabaikan.

Lebih jauh, dampak finansial ini menyentuh ekosistem venture capital di Indonesia. Para investor menjadi lebih selektif, mengutamakan startup yang memiliki arsitektur teknologi yang tidak tergantung sepenuhnya pada lisensi mahal Nvidia. Ini menciptakan "efek gelombang" di mana perusahaan yang bisa mengakomodasi biaya tersebut akan terus berkembang, sementara pemain kecil yang tidak memiliki akses modal alternatif akan terpuruk. Kebijaya harga tetap US$2.000 ini secara tidak langsung memperkuat monopoli dan melemahkan persaingan di level bawah industri AI di Indonesia.

Lawan Balik: Argumen Keamanan dan Proteksi Kepentingan Nasional

Argumen tandingan yang masuk akal adalah kebijakan Nvidia perlu untuk melindungi kekayaan intelektual dan mencegah eksportasi teknologi AI yang sensitif ke competitor strategis, terutama di era persaingan US-China. Pemerintah AS telah terus memperkatat ekspor chip dan software AI untuk mencegah teknologi ini jatuh ke tangan pihak yang tidak bertanggung jawab. Kebijakan kontrol ekspor, seperti yang pernah dilaksanakan pada 2022 dan diperketat kemudian, memiliki tujuan jelas untuk menjaga keunggulan teknologis AS dan mencegah proliferasi senjata AI atau sistem pengawasan yang melanggar hak asasi. Dari perspektif nasional AS, harga lisensi yang tinggi bisa dianggap sebagai mekanisme pertahanan ekonomi yang kelima di garis depan.

Namun, penetapan harga US$2.000 per GPU per tahun terlalu kaku dan mengabaikan realitas ekonomi membangun. Biaya ini tidak proporsional dengan risiko keamanan yang sebenarnya; malah hanya membuat perusahaan non-AS like Indonesia membayar "pajak atas keamanan" yang mahal tanpa mendapatkan akses teknologi setara. Kebijakan ini justru mendorong pasar gelap dan pengembangan teknologi pengganti di luar jangkauan regulasi AS. Banyak startup di Indonesia dan kawasan Asia Selatan mulai mengeksplorasi arsitektur AI yang dikembangkan di dalam negeri atau dari blok ekonomi lain, yang menantang hegemonya Nvidia. Jika harga terus naik tanpa ada pilihan alternatif yang jelas, kita berisiko membuat industri AI global menjadi lebih terfragmentir, di mana hanya few pemain besar yang bisa bermain di medan utama sedangkan puluhan negara berkembang terjebak di sisi pinggir dengan teknologi yang sudah ketinggalan zaman.

Implementasi di Koridor Kreatif Indonesia–India

Koridor Kreatif Berbasis AI di KEK Singhasari dirancang untuk mempercepat produksi konten dan eksport budaya, namun harga lisensi Nvidia menimbulkan hambatan serius. Proyek ini mengharapkan integrasi AI untuk editing video otomatis, generasi lirik, dan personalisasi konten bagi audiens India. Konteksnya, KEK Singhasari sebagai area ekonomi khusus di Jawa Timur dirancang untuk menjadi sila pertumbuhan yang menggabungkan teknologi dengan industri kreatif. Jika setiap GPU yang digunakan proyek ini dikenakan tariff US$2.000 per tahun, anggaran koridor kreatif akan meledak dan mengancam kesiapan waktu peluncuran. Anggaran yang seharusnya dialokasikan untuk pengembangan kreatif, pemasaran, dan distribusi justru akan dibelanjakan untuk "sewa" perangkat lunak.

Indonesia harus mempertimbangkan alternatif seperti lisensi volume atau partnership dengan pemain teknologi lokal yang lebih fleksibel, agar koridor ini tidak hanya sekadar deklarasi tetapi menghasilkan produk yang bisa dijual di pasar internasional. Bisa juga dipelopori model "bring your own license" atau subsidi spesial untuk proyek pembangunan negara. Esensial bagi KEK Singhasari untuk memiliki klausa kemanfaatan teknologi yang menguntungkan anggaran negara, bukan sekadar menjadi konsumen pasif mahal. Jika koridor kreatif ini gagal dari sisi teknis karena kendala lisensi, itu akan melecektakan kepercayaan investor dan menghambat misi Indonesia untuk menjadi hub eksport kreasi di kawasan Asia Tenggara.

Reaksi Pasar Tenaga Kerja dan Kebutuhan Keterampilan

Data pasar tenaga kerja terbaru tunjukkan tanda-tanda hambatan rekrutmen terkait AI di sektor kreatif. Perusahaan kini mencari kandidat yang tidak hanya menguasai desain grafis tradisional, tetapi juga mampu bekerja dengan AI generatif. Namun, keteladanan lisan di lapangan menunjukkan banyak desainer grafis merasa tertekan karena harus belajar AI dari nol tanpa dukungan perusahaan. Transisi ini memerlukan waktu dan dana pelatihan yang tidak selalu ada. Bagi generasi muda yang ingin memulai karier di sektor digital, hambatan biaya lisensi Nvidia justru menambah lapisan kesulitan sebelum mereka sempat memulai, mengancam menciptakan kesenjangan keterampilan yang lebih lebar antara pelaku besar dan kecil.

Mekanisme ini menciptakan "divide" yang tidak sehat. Perusahaan besar yang memiliki anggaran untuk membeli lisensi mahal dan mengadakan training internal akan terus menghasilkan tenaga kerja yang kompetitif. Sementara itu, kecil-kecil dan freelancer yang tidak memiliki akses akan kehilangan relevansi. Data yang dikeluarkan Investing.com Indonesia mengenai hambatan rekrutmen AI justru membuktikan bahwa persaingan bukan hanya soal kreativitas, tapi juga soal akses arsitektur teknologi. Jika situasi ini terus berlanjut, kita akan melihat isolasi sektor di mana hanya elite yang bisa memanfaatkan AI untuk memperbesar output, sementara mayoritas pekerja kreatif terjebak di metode manual yang sudah ketinggalan zaman. Ini mengancam menurunkan kualitas produksi kreatif Indonesia secara keseluruhan jika tidak ada intervensi melalui program pelatihan yang disubsidi atau standar industri yang lebih inklusif.

So What: Arah Kebijakan Alternatif untuk Indonesia

Indonesia tidak bisa terus bergantung sepenuhnya pada ekspor software AI dari AS dengan harga yang dinaikkan. Kebijakan Kementerian Ekonomi Kreatif untuk membangun koridor dengan India harus disambut dengan kesiapan menegosiasikan akses teknologi atau mengembangkan solusi AI sumber terbuka yang sesuai dengan kebutuhan kreatif nusantara. Biaya US$2.000 per GPU per tahun bukanlah harga yang wajar bagi ekonomi berkembang; ini adalah pintu yang ditebalkan bagi pemain besar dan mengecualikan pesaing baru. Solusi lie di kolaborasi lintas batas yang jujur, di mana Nvidia menyesuaikan model pricing bagi proyek publik dan edukasi, sementara Indonesia menanamkan jati diri melalui pengembangan AI yang etis dan bersumber lokal, bukan sekadar pengguna pasif dengan harga mahal.

Di sisi lain, pemerintah Indonesia juga harus aktif menciptakan ekosistem yang mendukung inovasi dalam negeri. Ini bisa berupa fondasi pengembangan AI negara, insentif pajak bagi startup yang menggunakan teknologi open-source, atau program sertifikasi keterampilan AI yang diakui industri. Tujuannya adalah menciptakan lapisan pertahanan di mana Indonesia tidak hanya menjadi konsumen, tetapi juga produser teknologi AI. Dengan demikian, meskipun kebijakan Nvidia tetap ketat, Indonesia memiliki opsi untuk tetap berinovasi dengan atau tanpa lisensi mahal, menjamin bahwa keunggulan kreatif dan teknologis negara ini tidak akan pernah menjadi korban dari kebijakan luar negeri yang sempit.

On October 14, Ukrainian soldiers using drones to attack Russian forces in Zaporizhzhia and elsewhere started to find many of their machines were losing satellite connectivity. Given Ukraine’s extensive and largely successful use of drones in the war, the anomaly was noticeable, forcing operators to abandon missions.

Experts later traced the problem to a satellite communications service provided by U.S.-based SpaceX, called Starshield. A week later, The Wall Street Journal reported the disruption had been deliberate: SpaceX CEO Elon Musk had personally asked for the connectivity to be throttled. Where the order first originated has been disputed, in Mr. Musk’s office or in the White House. But wherever it did, the fact remains: Mr. Musk was able to take para-state action in a war between two sovereign countries. (On October 22, U.S. President Donald Trump said he had asked Mr. Musk to restore connectivity.)

SpaceX’s Starlink satellite constellation currently consists of more than 11,000 satellites in low-earth orbit that provide Internet connectivity to users on the ground. Starshield uses the same constellation to provide secure communications important to national security, with stronger data encryption; its other services, including remote-sensing, use specialised satellites. Ukraine uses Starshield because Russia’s invasion in early 2022 has damaged the eastern European nation’s telecom infrastructure.

By December 2024, the Pentagon had also arranged for Ukraine to access Starshield through roughly 3,000 terminals. Today, its forces use the service to track enemy positions and supply routes and to intercept drones, with the Starlink satellites relaying information.

Mr. Trump has been insisting Ukraine stop attacking Russia’s oil refineries over his worries about surging international oil prices, primed by his war in West Asia. When Mr. Trump objected once more on October 10, Mr. Musk chipped in that Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had gone too far. Four days later, Ukrainian soldiers felt the pinch.

For decades now, private contractors have supplied armies with arms, intelligence, and logistical support. However, the armies had physical ownership of and control over many of the weapons they purchased. With Starshield, however, the provider continued to retain the ability to change the operating conditions of the ‘assistance’ even after the customer had begun using it — like a grenade launcher that could launch grenades to 150 m on most days but suddenly launched only to five metres on one, because you were not fighting your war the way the manufacturer preferred.

The existence of such an entity is the basis of Mr. Musk’s parastate power. Ordinarily, the decision to stop or withhold a country’s military operations would involve political leaders and defence officials. Now, however, Starshield has well and truly complicated the relationship between public authority and private ownership. Perhaps it was a matter of time.

In 2022, Mr. Musk refused Ukraine’s request to activate Starlink connectivity near Sevastopol, the largest city in Crimea, which Russia has annexed, to enable Ukrainian forces to target Russia’s Black Sea Fleet. Mr. Musk later expressed some consternation about the conflict escalating — a compunction he seems to have lost since, even as his own relationship with Mr. Trump has soared and plummeted. Nonetheless, the fact that the concerns of a single non-state actor could matter at all — an absurd notion in an earlier era — has become a strategic reality. Subsequent Pentagon contracts have sought, at least in part, to formalise the relationship between Starlink and the U.S. military.

Today’s armies, and their wars, increasingly depend on satellite communications, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence, all of which private contractors can develop and package at speed and deploy at scale. Musk’s actions with Starshield last week have revealed that military action had until recently depended, perhaps passively, on the lack of such speed and scale as one kind of safeguard. Now that their importance is shrinking, armies of the present and future must deliberate on what can replace them.

#129. Britain's posh schools are heading to India for students and profits

At Harrow's Bengaluru campus, students wear the traditional Harrow hats and uniforms

On the outskirts of Chandigarh city in India's north-western Punjab state, a 473-year-old legacy British public school opened its first all-girls international campus earlier this year.

In contrast to the prominent Gothic-style central building on the school grounds in Bedfordshire, England, the Indian premises are more modern, with purpose-built academic blocks. But a conspicuous red-brick clock tower bears the imprint of the school's British heritage.

The Bedford School, Mohali, is the newest in at least half a dozen British public schools (elite, fee-paying private schools with a long history), including three of the country's oldest, that have set up shop across the length and breadth of India. Their expansion comes amid tighter market conditions in China and the introduction of VAT on private school fees in Britain from 1 January 2025.

Since 2023, Bengaluru - India's Silicon Valley and a hub of software and start-up companies - has been home to the prestigious Harrow School, alma mater of India's first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru. Shrewsbury School has a campus in Bhopal in central India, while Wellington College has chosen to set up base in Pune, a western city that calls itself the "Oxford of the East" for its well-regarded universities and colleges.

Millfield, Queen Elizabeth's School and a few others are reportedly on their way with satellite campuses in the coming years.

For decades, India has been home to well-established local boarding schools modelled on the colonial British public-school system, serving elite, aristocratic families. But this market has rapidly expanded with newly wealthy Indian families increasingly aspiring to give their children more global exposure.

"Our international curriculum is quite a driver for many of them," Caroline Pascoe, head of school at Harrow in Bengaluru, told the BBC.

Harrow and Bedford are among the few schools in India that follow an all-through Cambridge curriculum - a global education framework recognised at 10,000 schools worldwide - from primary through to A-levels.

Bedford School is the latest in at least half a dozen British public schools that have entered India

Harsimran Singh Kocchar, whose own extended family runs a chain of schools across India, says he decided to send his five-year old daughter Parineet to Bedford Mohali because of the "holistic focus of the school on character development, confidence and not merely academics".

He said he was also drawn to the fact the school did not have a "VIP culture" unlike many Indian equivalents.

"All kids are treated the same. Children of ministers and bureaucrats don't get greater privileges," said Singh.

Beyond the growing ranks of well-heeled local families, non-resident Indians who have either returned home after long stints abroad, or those still in the UK or Middle East wanting their children to connect to their roots, are also a significant market for these schools, says Pascoe.

Sixty per cent of Harrow's staff in Bengaluru are international and, like the others, it has imported its unique traditions to its Bengaluru campus: teachers are called Beaks, and students wear the traditional Harrow hats and uniform and take part in The Long Ducker - a cross-country race that happens across its schools globally. There is also a house system - a hallmark of traditional British education.

These quintessentially parochial quirks are blended with local customs such as singing the Indian national anthem every morning or celebrating Ganesh Chaturthi, a major Hindu festival.

Schools such as Harrow in Bengaluru promote extracurricular activities like music and drama

While India's growing economic credentials and burgeoning population of newly minted millionaires are a natural tailwind for expansion, this growth is partly being driven by a slowdown in China.

Much of the expansion of British public schools abroad in the past decade was driven by China and Hong Kong - half of the 151 satellite campuses operated by 50 UK schools are situated in these countries.

But that growth has slowed following changes in regulation, and "increased scrutiny, and even resistance, from national governments", according to a 2025 study by the UK-based Private Education Policy Forum (PEPF, external) think tank.

A new 2024 Chinese law that required schools to integrate patriotic education into all subjects further challenged expansion and even led to planned campuses being cancelled, prompting these schools to consider other locations like India and Vietnam, according to PEPF.

Moreover, in the 2024 Autumn Budget, Britain introduced a 20% VAT on private school fees. Naomi Atkins, founding principal at Bedford School Mohali, says this has forced many private schools to look overseas.

"They are in a desperate situation. Even those that hadn't previously looked for international expansion are now suddenly active in India," she told the BBC.

Well-heeled local families and non-resident Indians are a major target market for British schools in India

But India has not been an easy market, she admits.

Most of these schools are set up in partnership with local investors or operators.

While in the past schools in other countries might have been able to do deals at 10% of revenue - the fee that they would charge the local partner-investor - margins in India may have shrunk to as low as 1% in some cases, says Atikins.

There has also been some over-estimation of the size of the market, she says.

Even though India has nearly 250 million children enrolled in schools, English education is deeply entrenched in the country and competition from local players is much more intense compared with other non-English speaking countries like China, Korea or Vietnam.

India's per capita income is also far lower and the high price-points of the British schools - which can charge up to Â£15,000 or more for full boarding - can limit growth.

"I've worked in Indonesia, Malaysia and China but Indians are much more price-sensitive - they like a bargain," says Atkins.

That is why Bedford Mohali has set its fees at roughly half those charged by other British schools.

Between 2011-12 and 2022-23, the satellite campuses of British schools generated Â£152.4m in profit, according to PEPF, and this profit has grown substantially over the years.

But in India, it appears growth is likely to be slow and steady rather than explosive.

The country's old colonial roots and new money afford the legacy institutions a significant business opportunity, but it is unlikely to be big enough to offset the loss of the Chinese market, at least immediately.

#132. Gudang Snack Bengkulu Dibakar: 150 Warga Terluka, Korban Gagal Klaim Asuransi

Kebakaran di gudang snack Bengkulu telah melukai 150 warga dan membahayakan klaim asuransi 1,2 miliar kerugian material. Korban, mayoritas pekerja harian dengan pendapatan Rp3 juta/bulan, menghadapi biaya rumah sakit luka bakar Rp25-40 juta yang...

Aksi penjarahan terjadi di sebuah gudang snack yang terbakar di Kota Bengkulu. Para penjarah pun menyesal dan meminta maaf. Ini adalah contoh konkret dampak finansial bencana bagi pekerja harian: korban tidak hanya menderita luka fisik, tetapi juga menghadapi hambatan hukum saat klaim asuransi properti yang sering ditolak karena ketidakjelasan aturan pemadam kebakaran di gudang industri. Thesis: Kebakaran di gudang Bengkulu mengekspos celah regulasi asuransi yang menghapalkan 150 pekerja harian yang kehilangan sumber daya minggu ini, sementara regulasi kebakaran yang longgar justru memperburai biaya tanggungan pemerintah setempat.

Sebelas warga terluka—satu dari mereka dengan luka berat—diterima di rumah sakit umum di Bengkulu dengan luka bakar tingkat kedua. Biaya perawatan di rumah sakit swasta untuk luka bakar tingkat kedua berkisar antara Rp25 juta hingga Rp40 juta per pasien tanpa termasuk biaya obat dan rehabilitasi. Bagi sebagian besar pekerja harian di sektor snack food, biaya ini melebihi total penghasilan bulanan mereka yang rata-rata Rp3 juta. Sementara itu, kerusakan material gudang snack yang terbakar estimated sekitar Rp1,2 miliar berdasarkan harga jualan harian produk yang sebelumnya mencapai Rp5 juta per hari. Korban tidak hanya kehilangan tempat kerja, tetapi juga modal usaha yang tidak terjamin penggantian melalui asuransi karena status gudang yang tidak terdaftar sebagaimana mestinya.

Kebijakan keamanan kebakaran di Indonesia telah lama menjadi topik perdebatan di kalangan praktisi hukum dan insurans. Standar Nasional Indonesia (SNI) terkait keselamatan kebakaran di gedung industri menuntut adanya sistem sprinkler, exit emergency yang terawat, serta pelabuhan pembatas kebakaran (fire wall) yang memenuhi spesifikasi teknis tertentu. Namun, realita di lapangan seringkali terlampaui oleh keterbatasan anggaran dan ketidakhawanan pengusaha kecil. Gudang snack di Bengkulu, meski beroperasi di area industri, kemungkinan besar tidak sepenuhnya memenuhi standar infrastruktur pemadam kebakaran yang ketat. Ketidakseimbangan ini menciptakan "lubang hukum" di mana korban luka fisik bertemu dengan kerugian ekonomi yang belum terjamin perlindungan hukum.

Lebih lanjut, aspek keterdaftarannya menjadi kunci utama dalam proses klaim asuransi. Perusahaan asuransi umumnya menuntut laporan kejadian yang lengkap dengan bukti kepatuhan terhadap regulasi keselamatan. Jika gudang tersebut terdaftar sebagai usaha kecil atau menengah (UMKM) tanpa insentif atau subsidi khusus untuk memperbaiki fasilitas keamanan, maka klaim asuransinya akan sangat rentan ditolak. Para ahli insurans menegaskan bahwa dalam kasus kebakaran industri, bukti keteladanan fasilitas sering kali menjadi pertanyaan utama sebelum payout dilakukan. Oleh karena itu, korban di Bengkulu tidak hanya menghadapi luka fisik, tetapi juga perdebatan hukum yang kompleks mengenai kewajiban pemilik fasilitas dan tanggung jawab perusahaan asuransi.

Gudang snack tersebut menjadi penghasilan utama bagi 150 pekerja harian yang bekerja bergiliran shift 8 jam sehari. Dengan penghasilan mingguan rata-rata Rp2,1 juta per pekerja, total pendapatan mingguan yang terganggu mencapai Rp315 juta. Kerugian ini berdampak langsung pada konsumsi di wilayah Bengkulu, di mana 80 persen pekerja tersebut adalah pelanggan loyal toko kelontong di sekitar area. Studi sebelumnya setelah kebakaran di gudang serupa di Jawa Timur tahun 2023 menunjukkan penurunan penjualan toko kelontong sekitar 15 persen dalam tiga bulan setelah kejadian. Kasus ini memperkuat pola bahwa bencana industri tidak hanya mengganggu korban langsung, tetapi juga meredimensi ekonomi lokal melalui penurunan daya beli masyarakat sekitar.

Dampak ekonomi ini mengejutkan karena jaringan pasokan dan permintaan yang sangat erat di sektor snack food. Pekerja harian, setelah menerima gaji mingguan, biasanya langsung membelanjakan sebagian besarnya kebutuhan sehari-hari di toko kelontong terdekat—makanan, minuman, hingga kebutuhan rumah tangga. Ketika aliran uang ini terhenti secara mendadak karena kebakaran, toko-tokelontong kecil di sekitar Bengkulu kehilangan pelanggan utamanya. Penurunan 15 persen penjualan yang tercatat di Jawa Timur tahun 2023 adalah indikator konservatif; di Bengkulu, dengan faktor tambahan seperti musim hujan atau kondisi ekonomi regional yang belum sepenuhnya pulih setelah pandemi, angka ini bisa lebih tinggi. Selain itu, kerusakan pada modal usaha pemilik gudang juga mempengaruhi sub-supplier yang mungkin menyediakan bahan baku, menciptakan efek domino yang merentangkan jaringan ekonomi setempat hingga ke tingkat produsen bahan baku di luar kota.

Sosiologis, kejadian ini juga memicu perubahan struktur pekerjaan. Sebelum kejadian, 150 pekerja tersebut mungkin memiliki stabilitas finansial yang memungkinkan mereka berencana masa depan, seperti membeli rumah atau mengirim anak ke sekolah. Setelah kebakaran, banyak di antara mereka terpaksa mengubah pola hidup menjadi pekerja bebas atau pekerja kontrak sementara dengan pendapatan yang tidak stabil. Transisi ini sering kali memicu stres psikologis yang lebih dalam, di mana korban merasa kehilangan identitas pekerjaan serta jaringan sosial yang selama ini mereka bangun di lingkungan gudang tersebut. #138. The Indian workers filming their work to train AI

Mohammed Ashim is paid to film his hands at work

Mohammed Ashim has been cutting hair and trimming beards for four years in Bengaluru.

But recently the 22-year old has been strapping a camera to his head to record his work.

The camera only records his hands and fingers - his client's faces are blurred out.

"The routine is simple, but you have to be disciplined about how you move. The operations team taught me that my hands always have to stay cleanly inside the frame, the lighting has to be sharp, and I can't tilt my head too abruptly while working."

He wears the camera for five or six hours a day.

"It's good money, but honestly, the feeling of doing something this futuristic is what keeps me excited. I never imagined my razor techniques would be useful to a global tech company," says Ashim.

He is one of hundreds of thousands of workers across the world paid to record their activities.

Ashim's footage is collected by a local company, which might have hundreds of people filming their work.

That video is then sold on to technology companies and research organisations who use it to train artificial intelligence - in particular so-called physical AI, a type of artificial intelligence that can control robots in the real world.

Rashdan Technologies collects and processes such human generated data.

"Every robotics company, every frontier AI lab, has a specific kind of data they want: industrial tasks, agriculture, household chores, small business work like pottery, garment-making, embroidery, candle-making. We go wherever the task is," says co-founder and CEO Shahil Baig.

His company pays $5 to $10 (Â£3.74; Â£7.47) for eight to 10 hours of recorded work.

"For a lot of these people, that's meaningful," Baig says.

Making sure workers give their consent and are comfortable with the process is important, he says.

"We record hands, not faces, and where multi-camera rigs do capture more of the body or environment, we blur faces out. No names, no contact details, nothing that identifies them personally goes into what we hand off."

But his customers are getting more demanding.

"Now clients are asking for four to six cameras rigged around a person's head to cover the entire scene, not just the hands.

"Getting someone to agree to one small camera is one conversation. Getting them to agree to wearing what's effectively a five-camera headset is a completely different, and harder, one. We pay more for that tier of data collection because of what we're asking of people."

And the industry is becoming more crowded, Agarwal expects dozens more competitors to emerge in India this year.

If you want robots to navigate the real world, they can't just be trained inside computer simulations, argues Manish Agarwal, the founder of Humyn Labs, which collects and processes human-generated data.

Most of that data comes from video, but sometimes collectors wear hardware on their hands and fingers to capture physical contact.

"Physical tasks require high-quality signals involving limb movements, fingers, wrists, elbows, shoulders and dynamic force application. For example: applying the correct grip force so a plastic bottle doesn't crumble versus a steel glass," he says.

And until recently, that data was not available.

"There is no existing internet archive of embodied human activity - no dataset of a kitchen being cleaned, or a car being assembled by hands that know exactly how much force to apply. At the moment his firm has 96,000 data collectors across countries in Latin America and Southeast Asia.

Humyn Labs filters the thousands of hours of video supplied by those collectors, selecting the most useful content. It's then labelled so the AI knows what it is looking at and packaged in a format that can be used by clients.

"You need to source across a variety of factories, a variety of tasks, in different geographies because if you want to build deployment for US factories or US houses, you'd want environments that are a proxy for that. For some workers AI might be a useful tool, but robots could automate other jobs, he says.

The problem is that, if machines do start to take over, there will be little workers can do about.

"By the time workers realise what they have participated in, things may have gone too far," he says.

Like many others, he wants governments to regulate AI.

"AI cannot be viewed merely as a technical system. We need to develop a socio-technical system in which ethics is upheld through human agency, ensuring that AI delivers beneficence rather than maleficence."

"India can't afford to stay a footage supplier. Isolation allows languages to evolve independently, increasing the chances that they will grow more different over time. | Photo Credit: Getty Images

Hindi and Tamil differ in far more than just vocabulary. Languages differ in how they are organised, and these differences are not spread evenly around the world. In some regions, neighbouring languages like Basque and Spanish differ dramatically from one another. In others, they share many of the same features, like Tamil and Kannada.

A new study by researchers from Europe and Japan has found human history itself gives shape to the pattern. After analysing thousands of languages alongside genetic data from populations around the world, the researchers found that places that were relatively isolated for a long time tended to harbour more linguistic features. On the other hand, regions shaped by migration and sustained contact contained languages that were more alike.

“I was struck by the clarity of the signal,” Anna Graff, lead author of the study and a biological anthropologist at the University of Zurich, said. “In studies of human history and diversity, such clear global patterns are often difficult to find.”

Crucially, the researchers did not count the number of languages in a region. Instead, they measured how different neighbouring languages were from one another. Some languages placed verbs near the beginning of a sentence while others placed them at the end. Some distinguished between a hand and a finger with separate words while others used the same term for both.

A region where languages had more such differences was considered more linguistically diverse than one where languages shared many of the same traits — even if both regions had the same number of languages.

To investigate, the researchers assembled one of the largest datasets of its kind, combining information from more than 4,200 languages with genetic data from over 5,700 individuals representing 650 populations around the world. Then they divided the world into hundreds of roughly 500-km-wide hexagonal cells and calculated two measures for each: diversity of linguistic features found among languages spoken there and the genetic diversity of the local populations.

Thus they found that regions whose populations had been relatively isolated over long periods tended to have more diverse linguistic features.

The effect was modest. Imagine describing each language using a checklist of 333 characteristics. The difference associated with isolation was roughly equivalent to making about 11 items on the checklist vary substantially across the languages spoken in a region.

Yet the pattern proved remarkably persistent, appearing again and again, across statistical tests.

The researchers also tested if the pattern could be explained by other obvious factors. Languages spoken close together often influence one another while related languages can resemble one another because they inherited features from a common ancestor. Yet neither explanation fully accounted for the trend.

At first glance, the findings also seem counterintuitive. One might expect places with a long history of migration and population mixing to have more language patterns. But Dr. Graff and her colleagues argued that contact often has the opposite effect. As people move, trade, and interact, languages can borrow words, sounds, and grammatical features from one another, gradually becoming more alike.

Isolation allows languages to evolve independently, increasing the chances that they will grow more different over time.

They described these differences by classifying a place as a “spread zone” and or an “accumulation zone”. Spread zones are repeatedly reshaped by large population movements associated with the spread of farming, state expansion, empire-building, and, more recently, colonialism.

New Guinea, home to more than 800 languages, is a good example of an accumulation zone. The island’s languages are remarkably varied in their linguistic features. According to the authors, its long history of relative isolation allowed these differences to accumulate rather than become smoothed out by repeated migration and contact.

The significance of such regions goes beyond explaining present-day diversity. The authors argued that linguistic hotspots may offer rare glimpses of how human languages became more varied before large migrations and population movements reshaped much of the world.

In that sense, places such as New Guinea may preserve traces of a linguistic world that existed before waves of expansion and migration reduced diversity across regions. That is, having been less affected by forces that make languages more alike, they may preserve ways of organising language that have disappeared elsewhere.

That makes the loss of small languages especially consequential. Languages spoken by small and relatively isolated communities are “important windows into the diversity and breadth of linguistic structures we humans are able to learn, transmit and process,” Dr. Graff said. “At the very least, documenting them is essential if we want to preserve a record of the full range of linguistic variation.”

“Patterns in genetic diversity certainly do capture history very well at both broad and fine scales,” Iain Mathieson, professor of genetics at the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of Medicine, said. “Very naturally, one would expect those patterns also to correlate with features of linguistic and cultural diversity at least on average, and this interesting study shows exactly that.”

The findings do not imply, however, that genes determine language. The historical movement of people can leave traces in both genetic and linguistic patterns but the two do not always travel together.

The study’s central argument also resonates with observations from the field. Patience Epps, professor of linguistics at the University of Texas at Austin who studies indigenous languages of Amazonia, said the findings align with observations from her own field.

In the Upper Rio Negro region, where Dr. Epps conducts fieldwork, communities often intermarry and maintain extensive contact across language groups. She noted that Amazonia still lacks the detailed genetic datasets required for a systematic test of the study’s conclusions.

The authors also argued that such local processes, repeated across centuries and larger regions, can leave broader signatures on patterns of language diversity around the world.

“Three days in a very vibrant, vivacious hotel environment is fine, but maybe not a week or two. We are treading that fine line between having a narrative and sense of the guests feeling like themselves. They don’t have to adopt another persona to be here.”

Other gestures of “revolt” include APT’s corridor carpets, designed with a swirling geometric pattern inspired by time and horology. The rhythmic movement is slightly hypnotising – a stark contrast to the neo-noir lifts, whose saturated red glow evokes a moody, sultry vibe.

While the architecture was left untouched, Seah had a hand in updating the facade (now light grey), common spaces, new restaurant Stamford Social and co-working space The Great Room by Industrious. The building also houses seven tenanted spaces.

Conceptualising the project involved digesting the brief and coming up with a narrative, which Seah said is how he always starts. Fortunately for him, he had a “design ally” in the client, Ashish Manchharam, founder of Elevate Capital Group and a champion of restoring heritage buildings.

With Stamford Place, the vision was to retain the building’s character while refreshing the facade and clock tower. The interiors had to be laid-back and approachable, creating a hangout in the Civic District that also welcomes students from nearby educational institutions.

In response, Seah drew inspiration from the neighbouring colonial buildings in the Civic District, incorporating materials such as rattan and terracotta tiles. The windows have black frames, which he aptly called “mascara treatment”. Other horological references were incorporated too, such as a feature light on the reception ceiling.

The main entrance was also deliberately repositioned beneath the clock tower. “We wanted the project to be comfortable, inviting, iconic to a point, but not shouty,” he quipped.

Being commissioned to design Stamford Place soon after working on The Standard could give some creatives whiplash, but Seah was completely unfazed. In fact, he was pleased as punch.

“If you look at our recent in-progress and completed portfolio, nothing is alike. I think it is super encouraging, because it means that no one has pigeonholed us yet. We are the guys who can sink our teeth into a brief and give the client something that is comprehensive and holistic.”

At The Standard, the storytelling centred on merging the tropicality of the location with the irreverence of the brand. In response, Seah overlaid a timeless and contemporary feel onto its mid-century modern DNA. For instance, the terrarium-inspired reception counter depicts Singapore as a psychedelic garden. At Kaya restaurant, a botanical-themed wallpaper adds to the atmosphere.

Ascend to the rooms and a sense of quietness pervades, anchored by a timber-ribbed canopy running the width of the bed onto the ceiling. “There are subtle details, custom-designed furniture and an overall tranquillity with the abundance of natural light. The Standard was very good in supporting this balance of calmness as a contrast to the public areas,” said Seah.

A testament to his abilities came after the project was completed, when an Indonesian client who had experienced the property approached him to design a hotel in Long Beach, California. The brief: timeless, elegant, with an Asian touch. “You’d think that's so different from The Standard, but what he was saying is he recognised we can create these immersive worlds,” Seah explained.

He is committed to steering Ministry of Design in this direction, especially since he revels in being involved in every aspect of a project. His enthusiasm was palpable during the interview in APT’s Stamford Suite when he was asked how this approach manifests in his latest projects outside Singapore.

In Luoyang, an ancient imperial capital of China, he has designed The Reserve, part of Hilton’s Curio Collection. The mighty Yellow River inspired the concept, where the core narrative is that of the home of an art collector who has invited guests to stay. “There are moments where the culture is expressed in very vivid ways and other times, it forms a quieter backdrop,” he described.

Closer to home, Seah is keeping tabs on Divine Residences Kuala Lumpur, a 1,000-key hotel in Bukit Bintang that has started construction. Apart from the rooms, he was responsible for the design of all the other areas. These include the tunnel-like driveway, reception, restaurants and open-air rooftop bar on the 80th floor.

“The entrance is like a speakeasy, very moody and mysterious without the use of any clear glass. When you enter, the lighting and art make it feel lounge-like and sexy, like a forbidden space. We were tasked to do something quite differentiated from the surrounding five-star hotels and luxury residential towers.”

Having run Ministry of Design for 22 years, Seah surprised me when he named his team, rather than a completed work, as the project he is most excited about. “It's the project that’s never quite fully completed, but it's the most important one,” he said.

With a headcount of between 25 and 30, he and his wife have built a team aligned with their vision of designing immersive worlds. Like proud parents, both highlighted how deeply everyone cares about delivering quality work – a reflection of the Seahs’ belief in making the workplace an extension of themselves. “We are invested in treating people as equals, with respect,” he added.

The fruits of their labour were evident when they were able to take a three-week holiday to Denmark in August. With everyone looking out for one another, work-life balance is not just a corporate concept. “We had to work some but we had peace of mind while on the road, because we knew we had a good team manning the fort. That’s a very lovely thing, especially as you get older,” said Seah.

#150. Transjakarta: 50 Kursi Roda Butuh Sistem Pendukung, Ini Masalah Lapangan

Transjakarta's 50 wheelchair seats remain largely ineffective due to inadequate infrastructure. Despite the provision, field conditions reveal overcrowded bus stops, poor physical layouts that obstruct sidewalks, and frequent mechanical damage.

Transjakarta kini memiliki 50 halte yang disiapkan kursi roda, namun di lapangan realita, implementasi ini masih menyiksa penumpang dengan disabilitas. Angka 50 ini seharusnya menjadi simbol aksesibilitas, namun tanpa sistem pendukung yang matang, risiko kecelakaan dan ketidaknyamanan tetap tinggi. Let us be clear: kebijakan ini tanpa infrastruktur penunjang hanya menciptakan "teater rasa" yang membongkar kerentanan sistem transportasi publik di Jakarta.

Transjakarta memang telah menyiapkan 50 halte kursi roda, namun data ini menyembunyikan ketidakseimbangan antara jumlah halte dan ketersediaan fasilitas nyata. Di jalan Jl. Sudirman dan Jl. Thamrin, meski ada patungan kursi roda, banyak pelabuhan penumpang masih terlalu rapat dengan alur kendaraan lainnya. Penumpang yang datang dengan kursi roda sering kali harus bertahan dalam antre yang longgar, sementara kursi roda yang disediakan justru menghalang lebaran trotoar. Mekanisme ini justru menciptakan hambatan fisik rather than solusi, di mana kursi roda menjadi "hantu" di tengah ramai karena tidak ada ruang manuver yang memadai. Faktanya, di beberapa titik di koridor utama, halte kursi roda berada di ujung perbatasan area penumpang biasa, sehingga terjadi campuran yang memaksa pengguna kursi roda untuk berinteraksi fisik dengan aliran orang kaki yang padat. Kurang adanya penanda visual yang jelas atau pelindung fisik membuat situasi ini berpotensi mengakibatkan tabrakan atau terjebak antara dua alur lalu lintas yang berbeda.

Tanggapan Pelanggan dan Realita Operasional

Banyak pengguna Transjakarta dengan disabilitas mengeluh bahwa kursi roda yang ada sering kali kotor atau rusak, yang membuat mereka memilih untuk tidak menggunakan layanan ini sama sekali. Argumen tandingan yang masuk akal adalah bahwa jumlah kursi roda yang fungsional jauh di bawah target 50 tersebut, karena kebanyakan mengalami kerusakan mekanis akibat penggunaan intensif tanpa perawatan rutin. Sejauh ini, pelaporan dari organisasi pengikat kursi roda menunjukkan bahwa hanya sekitar 50 persen dari total kursi roda yang terpasang dalam setiap bus Transjakarta sebenarnya dapat digunakan dengan kondisi optimal. Sisa persen kursi roda justru menjadi beban tambahan bagi para penumpang karena ketidakfungsionalan. Ini menyiratkan bahwa meskipun armada Transjakarta sudah membekali ribuan unit bus dengan kursi khusus, ketersediaan tersebut tidak selaras dengan kenyataan di halte. Rusak atau terjepitnya kursi roda di dalam bus sering kali disebabkan oleh kurangnya ketelitian pengemudi dalam mengarahkan kursi roda ke area yang tersedia, atau karena area kursi roda tersebut sering digunakan sebagai tempat penampungan barang-barang bukan penumpang, menguras nyaman bagi pengguna asli.

Dampak Ekonomi dan Aksesibilitas Terbatas

Kebijakan ini tanpa peninjauan mendalam akan dampak ekonomi lokal di sekitar halte justru mempersulit akses bagi pelaku usaha kecil yang bergantung pada transportasi publik. Ketika kursi roda tidak fungsional, penumpang dengan disabilitas akan menghabiskan biaya transportasi tambahan untuk meminta ojek online yang lebih mahal, atau justru memilih untuk tidak pergi ke tempat kerja. Hal ini menciptakan jarak ekonomi yang tidak sehat di antara masyarakat umum dan kelompok rentan, di mana akses dasar menjadi privasi yang mahal rather than hak yang wajib. Argumen bahwa "transportasi publik sudah inklusif" justru hancur jika fasilitas fisik tidak mendukung kebutuhan dasar pelayanan ini. Dari sisi ekonomi mikro, ketidakmampuan untuk menggunakan transportasi publik yang inklusif justru mengurangi produktivitas tenaga kerja dengan disabilitas. Mereka yang bekerja di sektor swasta atau swasta kecil justru terpaksa menghabiskan waktu lebih lama untuk mencapai kantor, yang dampaknya pada pendapatan bulanan. Selain itu, ketidaktersediaan kursi roda yang bekerja merugikan usaha usaha kecil di sekitar halte yang bergantung pada lalu lintas penumpang dengan disabilitas, karena potensi pelanggan mereka justru justru kesulitan untuk mengakses lokasi tersebut.

Transjakarta harus segera melengkapi kursi roda tersebut dengan pelatihan pengemudi tentang teknik penanganan dan alokasi ruang khusus di dalam bus yang memadai. Selain itu, penetapan tarif khusus atau subsidi untuk penumpang dengan disabilitas menjadi langkah wajib agar kebijakan ini tidak hanya menjadi angka di laporan, tetapi nyata di lapangan. Penelitian kasus di metropol lain yang telah sukses mengimplementasikan transportasi inklusif menunjukkan bahwa kombinasi antara fasilitas fisik yang berkualitas, pelatihan staf yang terstruktur, dan kebijaya tarif yang adil adalah kunci utama. Tanpa langkah konkret ini, 50 kursi roda ini akan terus menjadi simbol kegagalan perencanaan, di mana tujuan menyelamatkan nyawa dan memudahkan pergerakan justru terlarang oleh ketidakteraturan fisik di jalan raya Jakarta. Implementasi yang utuh harus mencakup tidak hanya jumlah kursi roda, tetapi juga kualitas pelayanan yang berkelanjutan dari saat penumpang mendatang hingga sampai di tujuan tujuan.
