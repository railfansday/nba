#2. Ebola: NCDC identifies more contacts of Abuja patient, Rivers case tests negative

THE Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) says it has identified and followed up 53 contacts linked to the confirmed case of Ebola disease caused by the Bundibugyo virus in the Federal Capital Territory (FCT).

The Director-General of NCDC, Jide Idris, a doctor, disclosed this at a media conference on Sunday in Abuja.

Idris said that the 42-year-old female patient had improved remarkably and was receiving medical care under isolation. Earlier report indicated that the patient had suffered multiple organ failure.

“The development was also contained in NCDC’s Ebola Disease Situation Report dated October 24, 2026.

“According to the report, the patient returned from Uganda through Addis Ababa on October 3 and developed symptoms, including fever, diarrhoea, generalised body aches and sore throat on October 7. She presented at a private health facility on October 10 and was admitted to another private facility on October 11,” he said.

Idris said the NCDC was notified of the case on October 19, after which an investigation was conducted and samples collected.

He noted that the laboratory confirmation was made on October 20 by the NCDC National Reference Laboratory. Also, the report showed that the 53 contacts identified included healthcare workers, domestic staff and the patient’s spouse, adding that contact tracing and monitoring were ongoing.

Idris said public health teams were working to identify people who might have been exposed to the confirmed case and monitor them for 21 days after their last possible exposure.

He urged Nigerians contacted by public health officials to cooperate fully and provide accurate information to support efforts to prevent further transmission.

The ICIR reported the Minister of Health and Coordinating Minister of the Economy, confirmed the Ebola case on Tuesday, October 20. He said 44 people who had contact with the woman had been traced as of that day.

Meanwhile, Idris said on Sunday while addressing journalists that the government had offered to pay every person placed in isolated the sum of N100,000 weekly to enable them to support themselves and their family members.

“We are paying for their accommodation; we are paying for their feeding. Some of them are asking for a stipend to maintain their families. We are providing N100,000 per person per week just to maintain their comfort and feeding, because they were complaining,” he said.

Quoting the NCDC’s Ebola Disease Situation Report further, Idris said the national Incident Management System had been activated at the highest level to coordinate the response.

“The diagnostic testing and laboratory surveillance were continuing, while genomic sequencing of the confirmed case had been completed by the NCDC National Reference Laboratory and the African Centre of Excellence for Genomics of Infectious Diseases. “The report also disclosed that a suspected case reported by Rivers State on October 23 tested negative for Ebola disease, with the result communicated to the state,” the DG stated.

On clinical management, Idris said the University of Abuja Teaching Hospital had undergone a needs assessment and optimisation exercise for readiness as an isolation and treatment centre.

“Assessments of infection prevention and control capacity has been conducted across states and healthcare facilities, while engagement with the facility managing the confirmed patient is ongoing.

“It has also strengthened its stockpile of personal protective equipment and other essential supplies, including the addition of 500 donated laboratory testing kits.

“Enhanced surveillance at the country’s points of entry is continuing, alongside public awareness activities and monitoring of rumours and misinformation,” he said.

Idris urged Nigerians to practise regular hand hygiene, avoid contact with the blood and body fluids of suspected cases, promptly report possible exposure and cooperate with contact-tracing teams.

He advised anyone who developed symptoms after possible exposure to contact public health authorities and follow their instructions before visiting a healthcare facility.

The NCDC urged the public to remain calm, avoid stigmatising affected people and rely on verified information from official public health sources. (NAN)

#3. “Penda Mbow, une intellectuelle engagée” raconte l’histoire d’une femme multidimensionnelle

Dakar, 22 oct (APS) – L’ouvrage ”Penda Mbow, une intellectuelle engagée”, rend hommage à l’historienne sénégalaise Penda Mbow, considérée comme une figure majeure de la promotion de la condition féminine, de la démocratie ou encore de la culture.

Ce livre édité par Présence africaine est décrit comme “un texte scientifique et classique” écrit à partir d’entretiens vivants et sans détours que les auteurs ont eus avec cette figure de la scène intellectuelle sénégalaise.

La cérémonie de présentation de cet ouvrage préfacé par l’écrivain Boubacar Boris Diop s’est tenue à Dakar, au WARC, Centre de recherche ouest africain.

“C’est une biographie critique que nous avons menée avec elle”, a fait savoir la co-auteure Fabienne Samson, chercheuse française à l’IRD (Institut de recherche développement), revenant ainsi sur la genèse de ce projet qui a duré trois ans.

Les auteurs de cet ouvrage coécrit par Mme Samson avec le chercheur sénégalais Saliou Ngom de l’Institut fondamental d’Afrique noire (IFAN), ont voulu aller au-delà d’un ouvrage conçu seulement pour rendre hommage à Penda Mbow ou restituer sa parole.

”Nous nous sommes rendu compte que les entretiens que nous avions faits avec elle étaient tellement forts, parlaient tellement de son vécu, étaient tellement imagés sur un Sénégal postindépendance, un Dakar très vivant, qu’on s’est dit que c’était vraiment dommage de perdre ces mots. On a voulu les mettre au cœur de l’ouvrage”, a-t-elle dit.

Le livre comporte trois parties, toujours précédées d’une introduction, qui replace dans leur contexte les paroles de l’universitaire, natif de la Médina, un quartier de Dakar. Il évoque aussi la trajectoire de la militante féminine et politique.

L’ouvrage parle aussi de la place des femmes dans le Sénégal, dans la politique, mais aussi des grands débats qui ont eu lieu, les grands événements culturels ayant marqué le pays, selon ses auteurs.

“Tout ce dont elle parle, nous l’avons mis en perspective, nous l’avons analysé dans un esprit critique, pour en donner un caractère scientifique”, a fait valoir Fabienne Samson, souhaitant que les jeunes générations lisent ce livre pour comprendre l’histoire et la place des femmes dans ce pays.

”Je pense que le témoignage de Penda Mbow est très fort pour cela”, insiste-t-elle.

Pour la professeure Rokhaya Daba Fall, ce livre dépasse la seule histoire de Penda Mbow, car il permet de raconter un parcours, mais aussi de questionner et de transmettre.

”Penda Mbow a fait le choix de l’engagement et de la liberté de ton. Un intellectuel ne doit pas rester à distance des préoccupations de sa société”, a-t-elle indiqué, donnant en exemple Mme Mbow, historienne de l’islam.

”Cet ouvrage est ma vie. Je me suis rendu compte que parfois aussi, c’est important de dévoiler un peu votre personnalité pour que les gens comprennent mieux”, a dit l’ancienne ministre de la Culture.

De nombreuses personnalités et universitaires étaient présents à cette cérémonie pour témoigner leur reconnaissance à Penda Mbow.

Historienne de l’islam médiéval, militante féministe, universitaire, Penda Mbow incarne ”la parole libre, exigeante et résolument engagée”.

#4. Kenya: Calls for Penalties Follow 12-Story Building Collapse

Nairobi — Nairobi Governor Johnson Sakaja has called for tougher penalties against rogue developers and the restoration of prosecutorial powers to county governments following the collapse of an incomplete 12-storey building in Eastleigh.

Sakaja said weak penalties for violating construction regulations undermine enforcement efforts and allow developers to compromise building safety, putting lives at risk.

Speaking as he led multi-agency rescue operations at the site along 1st Avenue, 9th Street, the Governor urged professional bodies representing architects, engineers and quantity surveyors to take action against developers who dismiss qualified experts after obtaining construction approvals.

He said some developers engage licensed professionals to secure approvals but subsequently remove them from projects and proceed with construction using unqualified personnel, undermining safeguards intended to ensure buildings meet approved designs and safety standards.

"We have formally written to the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions, and we hope our request will be considered. Counties must be empowered to take firm action against rogue developers who disregard building regulations and put lives at risk," Sakaja said.

The Governor questioned the deterrent effect of existing penalties, noting that some developers found guilty of violating construction regulations could face fines as low as Sh5,000.

"Even when such offenders are arrested and taken to court, the penalties can be as low as Sh5,000, which is not a sufficient deterrent," he said.

Sakaja argued that restoring prosecutorial powers to county governments would strengthen enforcement and enable authorities to take firmer action against developers who disregard approved construction plans and safety requirements.

He called on professional associations representing architects, engineers, quantity surveyors and builders to collaborate with county governments in identifying and investigating cases where qualified professionals are withdrawn from construction sites under questionable circumstances.

The Governor said the bodies should take appropriate disciplinary action against professionals found culpable, adding that experts responsible for building design, supervision and safety must be allowed to discharge their duties without interference.

Sakaja also reiterated the need for developers to obtain occupation certificates before putting buildings into use. He said structures must undergo the necessary inspections to establish that they are safe and fit for occupation.

According to the Governor, developers are required to secure county approvals, engage qualified professionals and sign indemnity forms committing them to comply with approved plans and construction standards.

However, he said some developers disregard these commitments after obtaining approvals, compromising the integrity of construction projects.

The calls for stronger enforcement came after an incomplete 12-storey building collapsed in Eastleigh, triggering a multi-agency search and rescue operation amid fears that people were trapped beneath the rubble.

The building was still under construction, with the ground floor occupied at the time of the collapse.

Nairobi City County emergency response teams were coordinating operations alongside the National Disaster Operations Centre, the Kenya Red Cross, the Kenya Defence Forces and other agencies.

Heavy machinery had been deployed to clear debris and create access to sections where victims could be trapped, as additional resources were mobilised to support the rescue effort.

Sakaja urged families and members of the public with information about missing persons to report to designated centres established by rescue teams at the scene.

#6. Nigeria: Ebola - FG Orders Strict Screening At Nigeria's Entry Points

The Ministry of Health says the directive follows concerns raised by the Office of the National Security Adviser over reported breaches of approved Ebola entry protocols.

The federal government has directed relevant agencies and health institutions to strictly enforce Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) screening and other entry protocols at Nigeria's designated points of entry following reports of non-compliance with approved measures.

The Federal Ministry of Health and Social Welfare announced the directive in a statement issued on Friday, following concerns raised by the Office of the National Security Adviser (ONSA) over reported breaches of the government-approved protocols.

The directive was contained in a circular dated 22 October and signed by the ministry's Permanent Secretary, Daju Kachollom, on behalf of the Coordinating Minister of Health and Social Welfare, Muhammad Pate.

#9. Does Nigeria truly have over 103 million voters?

AHEAD of Nigeria’s general elections in January and February 2027, Africa Confidential warns that “turnout in the elections is likely to be even lower than the average 26.7 per cent four years ago. Nigerian voters are among the least enthusiastic in the world…. Out of more than 93.4 million registered voters, 24.9 million total votes were cast. This represented an 8.03-point decline from the 2019 elections and was the lowest voter turnout since Nigeria’s return to civilian rule in 1999.”

Complaints about “voter apathy” are the staple of Nigeria’s electoral commentariat. The numbers and patterns seem to bear this out. In 1999, turnout was 52.3 per cent. Officially, it grew to 69 per cent in 2003; and has fallen since then to 57.5 per cent in 2007; 53.7 per cent in 2011; 43.7 per cent in 2015; and 34.8 per cent in 2019, before cratering to 26.7 per cent in 2023.

These claims require us to accord credibility to the underlying numbers from the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC). That begins with belief in the baseline of INEC’s electoral register against which turnout is judged. If that register is flawed, then all calculations hinged on it also suffer.

Yet, the foundational affliction underlying of Nigeria’s elections is not in INEC’s numbers. Rather, it is an absence of credible demographic management system against which to verify INEC’s claims.

The National Population Commission (NPoPC) claims 53 per cent of births in the country are registered and only 10 per cent of deaths are. The implications of this for the credibility of the electoral register should worry everyone.

To be sure, electoral rolls are ambulatory. People are not static: we move; relocate, and die too. This is why a demographic management system is essential. In Nigeria, this does not exist.

A register of voters does not automatically change because a person whose name is on it has died. But a person should also not be on the register one decade after their death. In Nigeria, there is no way of stopping this.

These define the many curiosities about Nigeria’s voting numbers. First, they bear no rational relationship to wider demographic trends. Nigeria’s pattern of supposedly precipitous collapse in voter turnout, for instance, is inversely proportional to both baseline population and civic gripe.

In 1999, Nigeria’s population was estimated to be 115,766,000. Nigeria’s current population is estimated at 244 million. That means the country has more than doubled its population since 1999 by 128,234,000 or 110.77 per cent.

Over the same period, the voting roll has grown by 81.4 per cent, even as life expectancy in the intervening quarter century has increased by about nine years. If life expectancy is growing with the population and crude death rates falling, then the rate of growth in the voting population should ordinarily outstrip the underlying population growth rate.

In over 20 years from 1999 to 2023, Nigeria’s population rose by about 87 per cent but the register of voters grew by 36,530,063 or 63.05 per cent. The mismatch between the two trendlines is significant.

In 1999, Nigeria had 57.94 million registered voters. This rose by 2.88 million or 4.97 per cent to 60.82 million in 2003 and then by a mere three-quarters of a million or 1.23 per cent to 61.57 million in 2007. By 2011, the number of registered voters climbed by over 12 million (20.5 per cent) to 73.53 million, representing an average yearly growth rate of nearly 5.12 per cent, where previously it had grown by 1.24 per cent in 1999-2003 and 0.3 per cent between 2003-2007.

By 2015, the population of registered voters fell to 68.83 million, a deficit of 4.7 million voters or 6.39 per cent, representing an annualised rate of reversal of 1.6 per cent. Yet, over the same period, Nigeria, a country with a median age of just under 18 years, had grown in population from an estimated 162.9 million to 181.2 million, an increase of 15.174 million or 11.23 per cent, representing an annual growth rate of 2.8 per cent.

The patterns from these numbers do not lend themselves to easy explanations. It is possible to speculate about what may explain this significant delta in growth between the general population and register of voters. Rational factors such as migration; agency dysfunctions in INEC; civic inertia or disaffection; or high transaction costs may be relevant. But there remain worrying patterns that are not easily accounted for even by all these.

As the Justice Uwais presidential committee on electoral reform pointed out in its 2008 report, much of what passed for electoral numbers in Nigeria before 2011 was voodoo. Just to illustrate this point, the INEC does not have a breakdown of official results for the 2007 presidential elections yet there are turnout figures for that election.

This leads to a second issue: Nigeria’s register of voters has always had invalid voters. The current register of voters in Nigeria dates back to November 2010 when the Attahiru Jega-led INEC set about establishing a credible register of voters for the country. The commission had limited time to authenticate the raw data before the 2011 general election.

When the Automatic Fingerprint Identification System (AFIS) finished its work on the 2011 register nearly four years later, INEC invalidated 4.7 million entries, which reduced the number of registered voters from 73.53 in 2011 to 68.83 in 2015 but not before these invalid 4.7 million were eligible to vote in 2011.

Third, the biggest problem may be dead voters. That’s an oxymoron because the dead should not vote. In 2022, INEC explained that it could not expunge the dead from the register because “the country does not have reliable data of births and deaths and the commission cannot engage in arbitrary removal of the names of individuals it suspects are deceased.”

This has not changed. It means we have no way of knowing how many ghosts there are among the 103.4 million on INEC’s current register. By failing to display the register as required by law, the commission does not even care to disabuse suspicions that its register may be bloated. Its response to non-compliance is institutional insouciance.

Nigeria’s register of voters is the classic Hotel California: it is “programmed to receive” and, for anyone with their name on it, “you can check out any time you like but you can never leave.” The implications of this for electoral integrity are staggering.

An illustrative exercise may help. Nigerian law allows adults alone to vote. The World Bank estimates the country’s crude mortality rate at 11.64 per 1000. Applied to a baseline from 2011, that is about 34,748,845 dead in 15 years.

But not all these will be adults. Mortality rate for under-5s is 117 per 1000 live births. That’s 700,000 to 855,000 under-five child deaths yearly. To 2025, that could be about 12 million dead under-fives since 2011.

The country also reported an estimated median of 111,425 deaths among 5–14-year-olds from 1990 to 2016. Applied again to a 2011 baseline, that’s another 1,671,375. In total, there could be up to 13.67 million infants, children, and adolescent deaths since 2011.

Adjusting the crude death rates for mortality among infants, children, and adolescents, there could be about 18-20 million people liable to be lanced from the electoral register since 2011. Applying the same methodology to a baseline of the 2023 register, that could be up to five million dead people alone on the register since the last election.

In its latest announcement, however, INEC said it captured a total of 10,772,421 new voters. It has not accounted for deaths or natural attrition.

Separately, at the end of 2025, the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre, (IDMC) reported that Nigeria had at least 3.7 million in internal displacement. The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees adds up to 478,000 Nigerian refugees outside the country. Admittedly not all of these are adults of voting age.

Almost assuredly, INEC’s 103.4 million voters is corpulent. When you look at the numbers on INEC’s register and account for the fact that the baseline data goes back to 2011, there is a reasonable likelihood that up to about 25 per cent on it could be dead or dud. That should put all the talk about voter apathy in perspective. But this story has a uniquely Nigerian sting: our diaspora on earth may not have a vote, but we may have a diaspora in Heaven with a veto.

#13. Nigeria: Health Is Economic Infrastructure - Why Nigeria Must Invest in Health

Listen to this article 8 min The recently concluded World Health Summit in Berlin, themed "From Crisis to Resilience: Innovating for Health", reinforced a key message: health spending is an investment, not just an expense. Yet Nigeria's 2026 Appropriation Bill proposed ₦2.48 trillion for health out of ₦58.47 trillion, about 4.2% of the budget. That is less than a third of the 15% target African governments agreed to in the 2001 Abuja Declaration.

Closing this gap requires recognising health as an investment in economic growth, much like roads and power. Nigeria's growth ambitions depend on people being healthy enough to participate in the economy. Health should therefore be treated as economic infrastructure in the country's development priorities and budget decisions.

Egypt's experience illustrates the potential economic returns of health investment. At the summit session Funding the Future: Unlocking the Economic and Fiscal Returns of Health Innovation, Khaled Abdel Ghaffar, Egypt's Minister of Health and Population, described a $240 million early-detection programme reaching 60 million people, followed by affordable generic treatment. It was projected to save $7 billion by 2030, a 688% return.

However, these are projections, not a confirmed result. For Nigeria, achieving comparable gains would require screening capacity and reliable medicine supplies that are not yet available at the same scale. Martin Seychell, Deputy Director-General at DG INTPA at the European Commission, cautioned that better outcomes depend on how the money is spent.

"We should not make the mistake of simply gauging and assessing efforts simply on the basis of how much money we are investing in health budgets. Because we know, of course, if you underinvest, you will not get good outcomes. But it doesn't follow that if you just increase the health budgets without a clear strategy, you will get better outcomes."

Nigeria's clearest return is household income

One of the clearest benefits for Nigeria is the funds families could keep if healthcare were more affordable. At the Health as an Investment: Preparing Systems & Societies for the 21st Century session, Dr Githinji Gitahi, Group CEO, Amref Health Africa singled out Nigeria when discussing the high out-of-pocket health expenditure, noting that what people pay out of pocket is "ridiculously higher than the government spends."

People pay about 71.9% of current health expenditure out of pocket. An analysis of the 2023/24 General Household Survey found that 45.5% of households faced catastrophic health spending, meaning health costs accounted for at least 10% of their household budget. These bills can force families to borrow, sell belongings or cut spending on food and education.

During the Strategic Health Economy session, Dr Ezekwesili recalled that evidence showing that malaria cost Africa about 2% of its GDP "changed the conversation" with ministers and described the disease as a "productivity tax on the economies." The Federal Ministry of Health and Social Welfare estimates that malaria reduces Nigeria's GDP by more than $1.1 billion each year, while Nigeria accounts for 27% of global cases.

At the same session, Dr Satoshi Ezoe, Japan's Assistant Minister for Global Health, explained that Japan reached universal health coverage in 1961 while still recovering from the Second World War, and argued that "we laid the foundation by making UHC, which led to socioeconomic development."

Nigeria is one of the first eight countries in the UHC Knowledge Hub, a Japanese initiative with the World Health Organization and the World Bank that brings health and finance ministries together to plan for universal coverage. Successful coverage should mean fewer families are pushed into hardship by medical bills and fewer workdays lost, not enrolment figures alone.

The economic case for health investment extends to building local industries and creating employment opportunities. Yet Africa captures little of the value from the health products it uses, as Dr Obiageli Ezekwesili, founder and chief executive officer of Human Capital Africa, told the summit during the session The Strategic Health Economy:

"[Africa's] capacity in terms of... global manufacturing... it's less than 5%. Then you look at... competitiveness... how much value does Africa gain from the health value chain -- also insignificant...

Nigeria aims to increase local production of essential health products to 70% by 2030. The Presidential Initiative for Unlocking the Healthcare Value Chain is supported by tax and duty waivers on pharmaceutical inputs and about $2 billion in financing commitments.

However, packaging imported products locally is not the same as making them locally. Manufacturers still rely on imported active pharmaceutical ingredients to produce medicines, leaving them exposed to foreign-exchange fluctuations and price volatility.

Local production also needs skilled workers, as does the wider health system. Nigeria has 95,456 registered doctors but only 60,551 with active practising licenses, while its 2024 National Policy on Health Workforce Migration requires wider implementation.

The role of policy in enabling health workers to deliver care also features in the summit session Health as an Investment: Preparing Systems & Societies for the 21st Century, where Dr Githinji Gitahi, Group CEO of Amref Health Africa, stressed its importance for community health workers.

Funding also must reach the services it is meant for. At the Health as an Investment: Preparing Systems & Societies for the 21st Century session, Dr Gitahi warned that prevention and vaccination budgets "are the first to be cut" when fiscal space tightens, because their results are less visible.

Nigeria shows the risk: in 2025, only ₦36 million of the ₦218 billion appropriated for federal health capital projects was released. For Nigeria, the value of health investment lies in the industries it builds and the workers it equips. Realising that value requires funding, procurement and regulation to support these goals.

“The failure to investigate the ₦78.8 billion cash-transfer scandal, account for the questioned funds and recover any unlawfully spent amounts violates Nigeria’s obligations to protect the human rights of vulnerable households,” the statement read.

In the suit, numbered ECW/CCJ/APP/42/26 and filed last week in Abuja, SERAP is asking the court to compel the government to account for the funds, investigate the alleged irregularities, recover any money found to have been improperly spent or diverted, and hold those responsible accountable in accordance with due process.

The organisation is also seeking an order directing the government to strengthen financial oversight and beneficiary verification mechanisms to ensure that vulnerable households receive the assistance intended for them.

According to SERAP, the questioned funds were meant to support more than 3.2 million households across 35 states and the Auditor-General questioned approximately N33.751 billion reportedly transferred to more than 3.2 million households across 35 states in 2023, citing the absence of adequate records to verify the payments and reconcile beneficiary information.

The report also identified N36.744 billion in payments allegedly made without prepayment audits and N4.616 billion in expenditures without adequate supporting documents, including other questionable transactions like N350.182 million reportedly spent on enrolment, N89.511 million for store purchases and N17.422 million in diesel advances.

The findings raise questions about the adequacy of financial controls, documentation and monitoring within the agencies responsible for administering the cash-transfer programmes.

SERAP argues that the failure to account for the funds and adequately investigate the identified irregularities could undermine the rights of vulnerable Nigerians to social security and an adequate standard of living, including access to food, clothing and housing.

The organisation contends that the government’s human rights obligations extend beyond establishing social protection programmes to ensuring that public funds allocated to them are properly managed and reach eligible beneficiaries.

SERAP further argues that inadequate beneficiary records, weak financial reconciliation and the absence of effective oversight could expose intended beneficiaries to exclusion from assistance designed to meet their basic needs.

It is relying on provisions of the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights and the United Nations Convention against Corruption, which address human rights protection, the safeguarding of public resources and transparent public financial management.

The organisation is also invoking Article 9(4) of the ECOWAS Court Protocol as the basis for bringing the public-interest action.

According to the suit, SERAP had earlier urged President Bola Tinubu and relevant authorities to address the Auditor-General’s findings.

In a letter dated September 5, 2026, the organisation requested a comprehensive account of the questioned funds, publication of relevant audit records, investigations into irregular expenditures and the recovery of any funds established to have been unlawfully spent or diverted.

It also requested payment and beneficiary records, verification documents and an explanation for the missing REMITA records, noting that the deadline it gave the government to respond had expired without adequate action, prompting the litigation.

The organisation maintained that the failure to investigate the findings and provide effective remedies could constitute a continuing breach of Nigeria’s international human rights obligations.

Beyond seeking a declaration that the government’s failure to address the financial irregularities violates its international obligations where protected rights are impaired or placed at risk, SERAP wants the court to order the relevant agencies to provide a comprehensive and verifiable account of the questioned funds.

It is also asking the court to direct an independent, prompt and effective investigation into the alleged irregularities, the recovery of public funds found to have been improperly spent or diverted, and appropriate legal or administrative action against anyone found responsible.

Other reliefs sought include stronger financial accountability, auditing, beneficiary verification, reconciliation and oversight mechanisms across federal cash-transfer and social protection programmes.

SERAP is further asking the court to compel the government to take effective measures to ensure that eligible vulnerable individuals and households receive their intended benefits and that any established harm to their protected rights is remedied.

The organisation argues that public funds allocated to social protection must be managed transparently and in accordance with Nigeria’s human rights obligations, particularly where millions of vulnerable people depend on the programmes for financial assistance.

No date has been fixed for the hearing of the suit.

#42. The gap between CBN account rules and Nigeria’s bank counters

In 2013 the Central Bank of Nigeria set up tiered KYC so that Nigerians without documents could still open a savings account. A 2026 field study of 100 branch visits across 18 banks found that what a first-time saver is asked for and told still depends on which branch they walk into.

IN January 2013, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) told banks it had taken steps to ensure that “the socially and financially disadvantaged persons should not be precluded from opening accounts or obtaining other financial services for lack of acceptable means of identification.” The circular that said so introduced the three-tiered Know Your Customer (KYC) regime, and its lowest level asks for basic personal details with no documents to back them up.

Thirteen years later, the counter does not always follow the circular. In 2026, Liners researchers walked into 100 bank branches across 18 Nigerian banks to open the account most people open first, an individual savings account, using valid Bank Verification Numbers (BVN) and National Identification Numbers (NIN). At 54 of those 100 visits, they were asked for proof of address.

That number alone proves little, because a bank may open a higher-tier account that lawfully needs more paperwork. The more useful question for a customer is whether a request comes from the rule or from the branch, and whether anyone at the desk says which.

The study was built to record what an ordinary first-time customer meets at the counter, using the same checks at every visit. Its full counts are published in the bank-by-bank tables on the study page. Every study figure in this article can be checked against them or worked out from them.

Method: Liners researchers opened individual naira savings accounts in person at bank branches, using valid BVNs and NINs. Most banks were visited by several independent researchers. At each visit, they logged:

Sample: 100 logged walk-in visits across 18 banks, in branches in Lagos and Ibadan, with a few in Ogun and Ondo. Visits per bank ranged from 10 at Polaris Bank to one at Keystone Bank. Branches were not picked at random; in the study’s own words, “Sample sizes are uneven by design; we went where a first-timer would go.” The study also logged 31 online openings at five digital banks; this article uses only the walk-in visits.

Limitations: In the study page’s own words, “This is a field study, not a statistically representative survey… it is not enough to claim a precise national rate for any single bank.”

For a Level 1, low-value account, the CBN’s three-tiered KYC circular asks only for basic customer information, such as a passport photograph and personal details, and adds: “Evidence of information provided by customer or verification of same is not required.” It also states that “No amount is required for opening of accounts”, and the same line appears at Levels 2 and 3.

Evidence starts at Level 2, which requires “Evidence of basic customer information” such as the customer’s name and address, with a passport photograph. Full documents come in at the top tier. For a Level 3, high-value account, “Banks are required to obtain, verify and maintain copies of all the required documents for opening of accounts”, and the account “is to be opened at the bank branches by physical presence of the prospective customer.”

The identity step has tightened since then. In December 2023, the CBN required Tier 1 accounts and wallets to have either a BVN or a NIN, with account opening to start by “electronically retrieving BVN or NIN-related information”, which becomes “the primary information for onboarding of new customers,” as The ICIR reported at the time.

On paper, then, a Tier 1 customer with a BVN or NIN needs no document to prove an address. So what were branches asking for?

Proof of address was requested at 54 of the 100 visits, and a utility bill in particular at 13. Nine of those 13 utility-bill requests came from just three banks.

The sharper pattern is inside individual banks. Of the 17 banks visited more than once, 14 asked for proof of address at some visits and not at others. Two banks asked at every visit (six of six, and five of five), and one never asked in five visits.

At least 27 of the 100 visits asked for proof of address without the customer being told which tier the account was on. In those cases, the customer had no way to know whether the paperwork belonged to a higher-tier account, which the circular allows, or to the branch’s own practice. Across all 100 visits, the tier was explained at 29.

The study’s published tables do not show which tier each bank opened, so they cannot show that any single request differs from what the 2013 circular requires of a Tier 1 account. What they can show is that the customer was not told.

The circular is just as plain about money. At 87 of the 100 visits, researchers were not asked for an opening deposit, in line with the circular’s “No amount is required” line.

At the other 13, a deposit was requested. Five of those requests came from a single bank, which asked at every one of its five visits for between ₦1,000 and ₦3,000. Two came from one branch, and six were one-off requests at six other branches.

Most branches matched the rule. The exceptions matter because they fall on the first-time saver the tiered system was written for.

The CBN’s Consumer Protection Regulations of December 2019 are clear about what a customer should hear before signing. Section 4.3.1 requires institutions to “Disclose all terms and conditions of a product or service on offer, as well as the features, risks, benefits, fees and other associated charges.”

For deposit products, section 4.5.2 adds the “minimum balance requirement and other limit on account balances or transactions where applicable” and the “monthly maintenance fee” to what the contract or letter of offer must disclose. In the study, transaction limits were explained at 16 of the 100 visits. At eight of the 18 banks, covering 41 visits, no researcher was told their limits.

Fees fared worse. Maintenance or SMS-alert fees came up at nine visits, and at the other 91 the researcher was told nothing about them.

In April 2026 the CBN circulated a draft revised Guide to Charges by Banks and Other Financial Institutions which, according to Nairametrics, would replace the 2020 guide once final and would require institutions to “draw the attention of customers to their rights to negotiate” where a charge is negotiable. The duty to disclose already exists in the 2019 text; the gap is delivery at the desk.

The barrier EFInA groups as institutional exclusion rose from 22 per cent in 2020 to 29 per cent in 2023. The 2019 Regulations already tell institutions to “Consider the needs of the financially excluded”, and the vulnerable groups they name include the “physically challenged and the non-literate”.

At one Lagos branch, a researcher described the account-opening form like this: “Someone who is not used to filling bank forms may find this one frustrating. The texts were tiny, and you may not know which sections to fill unassisted.” At 10 of the 100 branches, the entrance had steps and no ramp.

For a customer without a utility bill, or one who cannot read small print unaided, a proof-of-address request that changes from branch to branch is a bigger obstacle than it was for the researchers. That is an inference, since no such customer was in the sample.

Banks have a case. A bank official told Zikoko in 2022 that “The accounts that require a utility bill are the tier 3 accounts,” the highest level, and that a person without a home “can have a tier 1 account,” in which case “the bank marketers may just write down the address of the place they met the customer.”

Identity checks have also become stricter since December 2023. Asking for documents is lawful for a higher-tier account, and a bank may reasonably prefer to open one.

That case holds when the customer is told it is a choice. The 2013 circular allows a Tier 1 start, and a customer who hears that at the desk can decide whether to come back with a utility bill or begin without one.

The fixes the evidence supports are about saying the rule out loud. Banks could print the Tier 1 requirements at every account-opening desk, and write the tier and its limits on the confirmation a new customer takes home.

The CBN, which already sets the disclosure duty in its 2019 Regulations, could test branch practice through mystery shopping and publish the results by bank.

Thirteen years after the CBN wrote that no one should be shut out “for lack of acceptable means of identification,” a first-time saver still has to be told the rule exists before it can help them.

As a result, people took to the streets to protest Tinubu’s policies.

“In line with my constitutional oath to protect the lives and property of every citizen, our government will not stand idly by and allow a few with a clear political agenda to tear this nation apart,” the president said to the nation in his address on August 4, 2024.

Within the first few days, media reports confirmed that the police had tracked and apprehended over 800 protesters. By the end of the 10-day protest, security forces had arrested and detained 1,200 people across the country, according to the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC). Many of them were tracked down to their homes and places of worship with the assistance of advanced surveillance technology, according to the protesters interviewed for this report.

To spy on Nigerians daily, the government spends a huge amount of taxpayer money to procure these technologies.

According to the Institute of Development Studies (IDS), Nigeria has acquired more surveillance technologies than many other African countries, including mobile and internet interception, social media monitoring, biometric ID data, and ‘safe city’ monitoring of citizens in public spaces. They also found that Nigeria permits far more government agencies to conduct surveillance than several other African states.

“Nigeria is a far bigger market for digital surveillance technology than any other country on the continent,” says Dr Tony Roberts, Research and Emeritus Fellow at the Digital and Technology cluster at IDS.

CCIJ sighted a national security strategy document (archived here) from the immediate past administration of Muhammadu Buhari, in which the government clearly stated its objective of promoting “deliberate programs to develop advanced communications and surveillance capabilities.” It was version 2.0 of the Goodluck Jonathan administration’s national security strategy document.

This capability has enabled state security agencies such as the Office of the National Security Adviser, the State Security Services (SSS), the National Intelligence Agency (NIA), the Nigerian Police Force, the Defence Intelligence Agency, and the Economic and Financial Crime Commission (EFCC) to routinely intercept calls and listen to citizens’ conversations in gross violation of the privacy laws and international treaties to which Nigeria is a signatory.

CCIJ reached out to the Office of the National Security Adviser through its contact details, but it has not yet responded. An attempt to get an interview appointment was also unsuccessful.

Meanwhile, a 2021 report shared with the Centre for Collaborative Journalism, CCIJ, by Spaces for Change titled “Security Playbook of Digital Authoritarianism in Nigeria” confirms the government surveillance.

“The massive financial resources, equipment, and technologies originally procured in the name of counterterrorism and curbing insecurity have been diverted to monitor the movement of citizens, to track activities of civic actors online, to intercept private communications, to restrict online civic space, and to limit the ability of civic actors to organise, associate, and assemble freely,” the report stated.

In addition, CCIJ found that Nigeria allocated at least N127 billion to surveillance and security systems between 2014 and 2017, with about N46 billion allocated specifically to surveillance capabilities in 2017 alone.

BudgIT, a civic-tech organisation that facilitates budget reform, reports that the Nigerian security budget grew by more than 300 per cent from N1.5 trillion to N6.85 trillion between 2021 and 2025.

Part of that budget is spent on surveillance technology to fight against terrorism and banditry, though the result is marginal, considering the rising insecurity in Nigeria, the Afrobarometer survey confirms. Rather, the technology is used more to eavesdrop on private conversations and spy on ordinary citizens in their homes, especially civil rights activists, journalists, and opposition politicians who criticise the government, said Gbenga Sesan, executive director of Paradigm Initiative, a nonprofit organisation that builds capacity and defends the digital rights of Africans.

Organisers of the 2024 national protest came to this rude shock during the #EndBadGovernance campaign, which Amnesty International described as “bloody August.”

“When the security agents arrested me, they showed me all the phone calls I had made about the protest,” said Adaramoye Michael, popularly known as Lenin, the coordinator of the Democratic Socialist Movement (DSM), Abuja chapter. Michael, one of the key leaders of the #EndBadGovernance protest, realised while he was in detention that both his WhatsApp messages and phone conversations had been secretly harvested by the security agents.

The Police Force public relations officer, CSP Anietie Iniedu, has not granted an interview request as of publication. His assistants, who were also copied in the request, have not responded.

Ten other activists charged with demonstrating, along with hundreds of youths across the country, including minors, were arrested at different times by armed men from the Special Intelligence Response Team of the Nigerian Police Force (IRT), the State Security Services (SSS), and the National Intelligence Agency (NIA). The charge sheet obtained by CCIJ listed them as Michael Tobilola Adaramoye, Adeyemi Abiodun Abayaomi, Suleiman Yakubu, Opaluwa Eleojo Simon, Angel Love Innocent, and Buhari Lawal. Others are Mosiu Sadiq, Bashir Bello, Nuradeen Khamis, Abdulsalam Zubairu, and Daniel Akande.

Each of the protesters was tracked down with the aid of advanced surveillance technology procured from various countries such as China, Israel, Italy, the UK, the US, and Germany.

According to the Paradigm Initiative, surveillance often targets specific individuals and may rely more on account access than on sophisticated spyware. These could include SIM‑based monitoring or IMSI catchers, telephone eavesdropping devices used to intercept mobile phone traffic. Another targeting approach could be account compromise (email, iCloud, or Google) or spyware infections via malicious links or files. But most journalists, civil rights activists, and opposition politicians in Nigeria experience more SIM‑based monitoring that allows the security agents to listen to calls and read messages, said Sesan. Those monitoring methods match the surveillance technologies Nigeria has continued to procure over the years, he added.

According to the IDS report obtained by CCIJ, Nigeria regularly imports surveillance technologies such as internet interception technologies, mobile phone interception technologies, social media surveillance technologies, safe city technologies for surveillance of public space, and biometric ID surveillance technologies.

For instance, Daniel Akande, a member of the Network of Abuja Left Groups who participated in the #EndBadGovernance protest, had his mobile phone intercepted. He told CCIJ that police tracked him to his church in the Guzape District in the Federal Capital Territory, Abuja, after several days of hide-and-seek. Men from the IRT unit, led by an officer named Moshood, showed up during the church dedication ceremony for his four-year-old son. And before anyone could understand their mission, the men confiscated phones belonging to Akande and his wife at gunpoint and led him away.

For weeks, the police had monitored his phone calls with several others, including those who had nothing to do with the protest, before they were arrested. One was Chinedu Stanley, a driver who had worked with him seven years ago at a company in Abuja. He was apprehended three days before Akande’s arrest after investigators tracked him through their phone conversations. Investigators also tracked a female student and a pastor he interacted with during that time through their conversations. The pastor provided information that led to Akande’s arrest, he said.

“While in custody, I was constantly asked about my comrades. Although I denied knowing who they were, a policeman later told me that my call with Macaulay [another protester] was tracked two days before my arrest and that they were sure we knew each other. They tracked all of us using digital technology funded with taxpayer money, but terrorists are walking free in many parts of the country,” Akande said, highlighting the contradiction of government hypocrisy in the fight against insecurity.

General Tukur Buratai (Retd.), former Chief of Army Staff, in what seems like a Freudian slip, confirmed this claim in a recent TV interview: “If security agencies can locate ordinary citizens and influencers on social media, I don’t think they would not be able to find bandits.”

The Buhari government, under which Buratai served,  allocated N4.8 billion to the NIA in 2021 to monitor WhatsApp messages, phone calls, text messages, and other communications. Of this amount, the government allocated N1.93 billion to the “WhatsApp Intercept Solution” and N2.93 billion to the “Thuraya Interception Solution,” a communications surveillance system that can monitor voice calls, call-related information, SMS messages, and data traffic, among other forms of communication.

The spending came less than a year after the October 2020 #EndSARS protests, when millions of young Nigerians mobilised largely through social media and encrypted messaging platforms. Researchers found that the surge in surveillance spending is the Nigerian government’s response to that protest.

The reaction reflected the state’s determination to strengthen its ability to monitor digital communications and track future dissent, wrote Chibuzo Achinivu, a visiting assistant professor of political science at Vassar College in New York, in The Conversation. “Foreign suppliers of these technologies are motivated by both economic gain and influence in the region,” he added.

Government officials, however, denied the claim in 2017, presenting surveillance investments as part of broader efforts to improve intelligence gathering and address Nigeria’s growing security threats.

Roberts challenged this claim. “Maybe it’s time we started asking: In how many court cases does prosecution rely on surveillance data to secure conviction? Because governments tell us that these systems are necessary for our own safety, and we should be grateful because they’re going to reduce crime and catch terrorists.

And I think we need to hold them accountable for that claim. And the way to know whether it’s true or false is to see whether the use of digital surveillance tools, in fact, leads to the conviction of terrorists or serious criminals. The evidence that exists doesn’t support that thesis.”

Earlier, the Jonathan administration similarly spent billions of Naira on digital surveillance. Among them was a controversial $470 million (N75.2 billion) closed-circuit television (CCTV) project, which was widely reported in the media. The federal government contributed 15% ($70.5 million) in counterpart funding, while external borrowing covered the remaining 85% ($399.5 million).

The project, designed to support the Nigeria Police Force with nationwide surveillance and video conferencing infrastructure in Abuja, was contracted to two Chinese firms, ZTE Corporation and Hikvision, recently blacklisted by the US Department of War for posing a threat to America’s national security. But the project was hatched in secrecy to avoid public scrutiny. The former Director General of the Bureau of Public Procurement, Mr Emeka Eze, later confirmed to the National Assembly’s House Committee that the contract was awarded without due process.

Yet Jonathan’s administration spent more on security than initially discovered. As it later turned out, his national security adviser, Colonel Sambo Dasuki, was arrested over a $2 billion (N380 billion at the exchange rate of N190/$) arms deal scandal, with additional budget funding of $2.2 billion (N413 billion) and N643.8 billion.

This transaction is often a major source of corruption because of its opaque nature, Edeatan said. “An amount can be claimed as a cost, and that amount turns out to be heavily inflated. So there are again issues of corruption as officials use this contract as a means of siphoning resources.”

The list includes Egypt, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE. Others include Afghanistan, Bahrain, China, India, Indonesia, Iraq, Kenya, Lebanon, Malaysia, Russia, South Korea, Singapore, Thailand, Turkey, and Venezuela. These devices have specific functionality permitting filtering, censorship, and surveillance.

A CCIJ analysis of the Nigerian budget from 2016 to 2026 shows the Nigerian government allocated N1.4 trillion in capital budget to four key agencies that reportedly spy on citizens. They are the SSS (N218.4 billion), NIA (N117.2 billion), DIA (N117.4 billion), ONSA (N551.4 billion), and the police (N386.5 billion).

An earlier IDS report found that Nigeria spent more than $2 billion (N990 billion at an exchange rate of N495/$) on surveillance technologies between 2013 and 2022, making it one of Africa’s largest purchasers of digital monitoring tools. The technologies acquired over that period include systems capable of intercepting communications, analysing digital data, and identifying the location of mobile devices.

During the #EndBadGovernance protests, security agencies used some of these capabilities to identify and locate protest organisers and participants, according to security officials and documents reviewed by CCIJ. The Nigerian government often justifies the heavy spending as a national security measure.

But for many Nigerians, billions invested in surveillance systems have not improved security. Parents still worry when their children go to school; farmers fear attacks on their fields, and commuters remain vulnerable to kidnappers on major highways. Nigeria ranks high among the countries most severely affected by terrorism, according to the Global Terrorism Index (GTI).

Terrorist attacks rose from 120 incidents in 2024 to 171 in 2025, even as government security spending reached record levels. Yet the security agents are often swift to monitor and arrest activists, journalists, opposition politicians, or ordinary people who dare to criticise the government. Africa’s common position seeks at least two permanent seats with the same prerogatives as existing permanent members, including veto rights if retained, alongside five non-permanent seats.

The continent’s advocates cite its geographical size, development needs and strategic resources, including approximately 30 per cent of global mineral reserves, as reasons for stronger representation.

Reforming the security council requires a UN Charter amendment approved by two-thirds of general assembly members and ratified by two-thirds of UN member states, including all five permanent members.
