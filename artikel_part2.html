#60. Attack on actor Tiger Shroff is dark reminder of Bollywood's gangster past

Actor Tiger Shroff, who made his debut in 2014, is known for his action roles

On Sunday evening, Bollywood actor Tiger Shroff went to play football in Juhu, a leafy, affluent Mumbai neighbourhood where film stars live, fans congregate and celebrity sightings are a part of life. But as the 36-year-old was leaving the ground in his BMW, shots rang out. His driver was wounded, as was a vlogger who had come to film the actor.

Shroff, whose films usually include elaborate action sequences and choreographed escapes, had just survived an actual shooting attempt.

Four days later, the case still dominates headlines in India, with each new revelation spawning fresh theories, competing claims and a growing list of alleged conspiracies.

Police have detained or arrested 10 suspects, with investigators tracing alleged weapon deliveries through several states and examining payments made to the alleged attackers.

The attempted shooting has also brought the name of Lawrence Bishnoi, one of India's most notorious gangsters, back into the headlines. Soon after the attack, a screenshot of a social media post purportedly made by an alleged associate of the Bishnoi gang circulated online, claiming responsibility. A separate audio recording emerged in which a man identifying himself as Anil Pandit repeated the claim and said Shroff had been targeted over his alleged links to actor Salman Khan, who Bishnoi has been threatening for years.

Mumbai police have not verified the claims or established that the Bishnoi gang was behind the attempted shooting. Investigators are still trying to determine who ordered the attack and why.

The targeting of Shroff follows a series of threats and attacks involving prominent Bollywood figures, including gunfire outside Khan's home in 2024 and filmmaker Rohit Shetty's house earlier this year. Together, the incidents have revived fears about the reach of organised crime into the film industry, stirring memories of a darker era in Mumbai's history.

During the 1990s and early 2000s, the city's underworld exerted formidable influence over the film industry, financing productions, demanding protection money and sometimes using violence to enforce its demands. It took years of police operations, and the gradual arrival of institutional financing, to loosen that grip.

Now, as fresh allegations and theories emerge almost daily, a question that once seemed to belong to Mumbai's past has returned: is the underworld trying to control Bollywood again?

Those who confronted organised crime in Mumbai at its peak say the answer is not as straightforward as the attempt to shoot Shroff might suggest.

D Sivanandhan, who took charge of Mumbai's Crime Branch in 1998, remembers a time when a phone call from Dubai, where some of the most notorious gang leaders were based, could decide the fate of a film - who starred in it, financed it or profited from its distribution.

In an industry dependent on private money, the line between investor and extortionist could be perilously thin. And the threats were real. In 1997, music mogul Gulshan Kumar was shot dead outside a Mumbai temple. Three years later, filmmaker Rakesh Roshan survived an assassination attempt.

Sivanandhan investigated the financing of Chori Chori Chupke Chupke, a Salman Khan film whose producer and his assistant were each sentenced to six years in prison under Maharashtra's organised crime law for underworld links. The film's financier received a one-year sentence for withholding information about those links. Police seized the film's negatives, and its release eventually happened under court supervision.

Gangsters were not always imposing themselves on a reluctant industry. Some filmmakers willingly sought their money, Sivanandhan recalled, in arrangements that could benefit both sides and proved difficult for police to dismantle.

But Sivanandhan is reluctant to see the latest violence as a return of the mafia he once fought. The men he pursued - Dawood Ibrahim, Chhota Shakeel, Arun Gawli and others - belonged to criminal networks rooted in Mumbai, even when their leaders operated from abroad.

"This is not the Mumbai mafia which we know in the last 60 years," he said. "This is something else."

Gangster Lawrence Bishnoi has been locked up in prison since 2015

That something else has become inseparable from the name Lawrence Bishnoi. A gangster with roots in northern India who has been in jail for years, Bishnoi has acquired an aura of folkloric menace: a prisoner said to command gunmen across continents, his name invoked in threats and claims of responsibility that spread almost instantaneously online.

Bishnoi has publicly threatened Khan over a 1998 poaching case involving blackbucks, a species of antelope revered by the Bishnoi community. In April 2024, gunmen fired five rounds outside Khan's Mumbai home. Six months later, Baba Siddique, a politician close to Khan and other actors, was murdered. Mumbai police have linked both attacks to the Bishnoi gang, though they have not established any personal involvement by Lawrence Bishnoi in Siddique's killing.

Some allegations against his network are backed by police investigations; others remain untested. But the distinction between what the gang has demonstrably done and what is claimed in its name can become blurred in the public imagination, doing little to diminish Bishnoi's notoriety.

Rakesh Maria, a former Mumbai police commissioner, has cited Bishnoi as an example of a new generation of gangsters who are more technologically adept and internationally connected than their predecessors. "The old underworld was visible and territorial, but the new one is dispersed, fluid, making it harder to pin down," Maria told The Print, external last year. "They no longer need to reside in the city to command it."

Bishnoi's gang claimed responsibility for killing Baba Siddiqui (right) in 2024, citing his ties to Salman Khan (left)

The relationship between gangsters and Bollywood has changed in another way, too, in who gets to shape their image.

In the 1990s, Bollywood itself helped turn Mumbai's feared underworld figures into objects of fascination. Films such as Satya (1998) and Company (2002) brought a gritty, visceral realism to their world, while Manoj Bajpayee's portrayal of gangster Bhiku Mhatre in Satya became, in the words of film scholar Ashvin Immanuel Devasundaram, "mythologised and talismanic".

The fear and fascination of that era were "seared into the collective consciousness", Devasundaram told the BBC.

Each new attack brings those memories back, he added. But today's gangsters no longer need cinema to build their mythology. Social media allows them to shape their own image, circulating threats, claims of responsibility and carefully staged photographs to audiences far beyond Mumbai.

After the latest shooting, police reportedly stepped up security outside the homes of Khan and Rohit Shetty. The All Indian Cine Workers Association demanded greater protection, asking: "Today it is Tiger Shroff. Who is next?"

There is little evidence so far that criminal networks have regained their former influence over Bollywood's finances and decisions. But the latest attacks have exposed a different vulnerability.

Gangsters may no longer need to control an industry to unsettle it. A single shooting attempt, amplified across millions of phone screens, can spread fear far beyond its intended target.

Sivanandhan, who spent years confronting Mumbai's gangsters, knows the limits of even the tightest security.

"There is always a crack in the armour," he said.

Keberadaan Gubernur DKI Pramono Anung yang hadir untuk meresmikan GMAHK Taman Harapan memang menciptakan perubahan fisik di kawasan ini, namun bagi warga setempat, momen ini menimbulkan pertanyaan mendasar: apakah ruang hijau ini benar-benar terbuka bagi semua lapisan masyarakat atau hanya sekadar elemen estetika bagi pengelola?

Pramono Anung: DKI Rumah bagi Seluruh Warga

Secara resmi, deklarasi gubernur ini diutarai melalui acara pemotongan selai di Taman Harapan. Pramono Anung menegaskan, "Jakarta adalah rumah bagi seluruh warga untuk memperoleh hak yang sama tanpa diskriminasi terhadap suku, agama, dan budaya." Namun, di lapangan, respons masyarakat bervariasi. Ketua RT 05 RW 08 di Kecamatan Kebayoran Baru, Bapak Soleh, mengaku terkejut akan keberadaan fasilitas ini di tengah kawasan yang hingga lama dikenal dengan masalah aksesibilitas. "Sebelumnya area ini cukup gelap malam hari. Harapannya dengan Taman Harapan, kegelapan bisa diatasi dan anak-anak kawasan ini punya tempat bermain yang aman," ujar Soleh sambil menunjukkan area taman yang masih kosong dari pelayanan publik.

Di sisi lain, tokoh masyarakat agama di sekitar kawasan Palmerah, Ustadz Fachri, memberikan komentar lebih kritis. "Kita menghargai inisiatif hijau, tetapi lebih penting lagi pastikan fasilitas ini tidak mengekslusif. Harus ada jaminan bahwa pelinggam maupun pedagang kaki lima masih bisa beraktivitas di sini tanpa takut disingkirkan," ujarnya. Kedua respons ini mencerminkan dua sisi dari visi gubernur: sisi optimisme terhadap pengembangan kota dan sisi praktis mengenai akses dan inklusivitas di level jalan.

Lapangan: Dari Polusi Udara ke Ruang Hijau

Kehadiran Taman Harapan ini tidak lepas dari konteks sebelumnya mengenai kondisi lingkungan Jakarta. Hanya dua hari sebelumnya, pada 23 Oktober, gubernur Pramono telah melaporkan penurunan polusi udara setelah Operasi Memeriksa Cek (OMC) berlangsung dua hari penuh. "Kondisi polusi udara di Jakarta menurun setelah dilakukan OMC dua hari," ujar Pramono melalui ANTARA. Penurunan ini menjadi patokan utama mengapa proyek seperti Taman Harapan dianggap krusial. Lingkungan yang lebih bersih justru menjadi komoditas yang dituntut warga setempat, bukan sekadar "plus" estetika.

Secara spesifik, kawasan Kompleks GMAHK sebelumnya dikenal dengan laju penumpukan sampah dan air stagnan saat musim hujan. Bapak Soleh dari RT 05 RW 08 menambahkan, "Kita lihat sendiri airnya mengalir dengan lebih baik setelah pemasangan tanaman tertentu di tepi taman. Isu inklusif: Antara Harapan dan Realita Pasar

Pasar Minggu, yang berada sekitar 2 kilometer dari lokasi ini, adalah sumber hidup bagi ribuan pedagang. Apakah Taman Harapan akan menciptakan persaingan atau kerjasama? Sebaliknya, wakil Gubernur Bidang Masyarakat dan Kebudayaan, Irmawati, mengaku sudah ada mekanisme penetapan jadwal dan area khusus bagi pedagang. "Kami akan menetapkan zona pedagang yang tetap beroperasi di tepi Taman Harapan, bukan di dalam area taman itu sendiri. Tujuannya adalah saling menghormati ruang dan kebersihan," ujar Irmawati. Mekanisme ini sebenarnya mencoba menyeimbangkan antara visi "rumah bagi seluruh warga" dengan realita ekonomi yang harus berkelanjutan.

Di balik keberhasilan meresmikan, tantangan terbesar kini adalah menjaga keberlangsungan fasilitas ini. Biaya pemeliharaan taman hijau di area urban seperti DKI Jakarta sering menjadi beban bagi anggaran daerah. Menurut laporan dari Balai Penyelenggaraan Taman Kota Jakarta, anggaran tahunan untuk pemeliharaan ratusan taman di DKI Jakarta tahun 2026 hanya mencakup 60 persen dari target yang dibutuhkan untuk menjaga kualitas tanam dan fasilitas.

Pramono Anung sendiri mengakui hal ini selama sidang pers. "Kami akan memaksimalkan partisipasi masyarakat dalam menjaga kebersihan. Masyarakatisasi adalah kunci," katanya. Ia juga menjanjikan bahwa akan ada mekanisme pengawasan berkala yang melibatkan kelompok warga dan pemangku kepentingan lokal. "Setiap bulannya, akan ada rapat koordinasi antara Dinas Pariwisata, Dinas Lingkungan Hidup, dan Kepala RT/RW untuk mengevaluasi kondisi," ujar gubernur tersebut. Langkah ini sebenarnya adalah respons terhadap kritik sebelumnya mengenai ketidaktahanan pemeliharaan oleh aparatur.

Resmikan GMAHK Taman Harapan memang menjadi momen penting bagi DKI Jakarta sebagai upaya memperbaiki citra kota yang lebih inklusif dan bersih. Namun, kata-kata gubernur Pramono Anung tentang "Jakarta adalah rumah bagi seluruh warga" hanya akan menjadi kenyataan jika mekanisme operasional, perlindungan hak pedagang, dan pemeliharaan teratur bisa diterapkan dengan konsisten di lapangan. Untuk ibu Karti di Pasar Minggu dan ribuan pedagang di sekitarnya, hasil dari proyek ini akan diukur bukan dari keindahan patung atau tanaman, tapi dari kenyamanan beraktivitas sehari-hari tanpa diskriminasi dan dengan lingkungan yang sebenarnya lebih sehat. The language of world politics has become remarkably predictable. Every military strike, diplomatic initiative, economic sanction, or strategic partnership is justified in the name of national interest. Television debates and commentaries have a common vocabulary — power, deterrence, balance of power, strategic autonomy, and security. It almost appears as though International Relations has become an emotionless discipline, focused on power concentration, where morality and ethics have little place. Yet the defining geopolitical crises of recent years tell a very different story.

International Relations, both as an academic discipline and as a practice of diplomacy, has been largely shaped by Western intellectual traditions, with much of academic research originating in the United States of America. No wonder, famous IR scholar, Stanley Hoffman, in his article, argued that IR is an American Social Science. It is difficult to refute Hoffman’s argument because initially the modern international order emerged when European powers dominated much of the world, leaving the Global South with little role in designing the institutions, norms, and rules that continue to govern global politics. Realism portrays the international system as anarchic and argues that states, in the absence of a central authority, are compelled to pursue their national interests. Over time, Realism travelled from classrooms to newsrooms, becoming the default language through which foreign policy is interpreted.

At the ninth World AI Conference in Shanghai this month, Chinese President Xi Jinping addressed the gathering for the first time in nine years. It is indicative of the priority that AI occupies for the leadership. At the conference, Mr. Xi pitched Chinese AI models as a global public good. The contrast was clear as day. America is selling closed-source, proprietary models while China is offering open-weight models to the world. This distinction is not merely technical, but equally political.

Unlike Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, which host their frontier models on their respective servers and offer services via an application programming interface (API), Chinese labs such as DeepSeek, Qwen, and Moonshot also release their weights for anyone to download. Unlike American models, these can be customised, modified, and even further trained. There is no subscription to cancel or terms of service. The maker cannot revoke or restrict access. So long as one can manage the requisite compute to host the model, even Papua New Guinea or Senegal can run it locally on their servers. And most importantly, the data never leaves the border.

However, one may wonder what explains China’s benevolence. Well, to begin with, the objective is to project China as a responsible AI player in the comity of nations. Unlike the Americans, who he alleged to have overstretched the concept of national security to AI, and thereby adopted an exclusionary approach, China is committed to sharing the benefits and fruits of AI. Mr. Xi’s framing was indicative in this regard. He described AI as “humanity’s collective wisdom,” a “historic opportunity” to “bridge the AI and digital divides,” and something that “should not be a solo performance by a single country but a symphony of international cooperation.” The underlying message, aimed at Washington, projected Beijing as the open and cooperative alternative to the U.S.’s AI models.

But there is a limiting factor at play too that explains Beijing’s benevolence — its compute constraint, both for training and inference.

Since 2022, the U.S. administration, in concert with its allies, has imposed a series of export restrictions on China, limiting its ability to both mass-import and mass-manufacture high-end chips.

To understand this constraint, one must separate two very different tasks — training a model and serving it. Training is a one-time cost. A lab assembles a cluster of chips, runs it for a few months and comes up with a finished model. The fleet required is numerically small. For instance, GPT-4 was trained on an estimated 25,000 Nvidia chips, and Meta trained its Llama 3.1 on ~16,400 chips. DeepSeek, on the other hand, trained its acclaimed V3 model on ~2,000 export-compliant H800s. A frontier model, in other words, needs chips in the thousands, and it needs them only once.

Serving that model to the public, however, is an altogether different proposition. Inference scales with the user base and requires continuous expansion as it must answer hundreds of millions of user queries. OpenAI crossed a million GPUs in 2025, and Meta targeted the equivalent of 1.3 million by the end of 2025. Thus, for any model with a mass consumer base, the serving fleet dwarfs the training fleet, often by ten to a hundred times.

In other words, the distinction is that of a stockpile and a flow. To train a model, a lab can accumulate ten or twenty thousand chips through smuggling, stockpiling, and modest domestic production. This is precisely why the export controls could not prevent China from reaching parity at the model level. Inference, however, cannot be solved this way. The export controls mean that the most advanced chips remain out of bounds for Chinese firms. Additionally, the concessions the U.S. has offered exist largely on paper. Around 10 Chinese firms were cleared in principle to buy up to 75,000 H200 chips each, but not a single unit had been delivered by mid-2026, as sales remained stalled and Beijing itself directed firms towards domestic alternatives.

China can attempt to bridge the shortfall through domestic production, but even here, restrictions on export of EUVs continue to prove a handicap. SMIC’s 5nm line in China runs at roughly 20% yield without EUV tools, far short of commercial viability. This severely restricts the ability of Chinese labs to offer services via API alone. DeepSeek’s decision to freeze new registrations in January 2025 was influenced by a surge in traffic it could not serve, alongside a reported ‘large-scale malicious attack’.

Furthermore, given that Chinese models are nearly as good as the U.S.’s frontier models, their popularity is increasing. Data show that the Chinese models’ share of U.S. firms’ AI usage has hit a record 60% on OpenRouter — a marketplace for routing queries to various models based on requests.

American firms are preferring Chinese models as they provide comparable services at a fraction of the cost. But this success comes with a rider — the need for enormous compute. The solution thus is to simply outsource compute via open-weight models. Hence, China’s open-weight offering to the world is largely a product of its structural constraint. It can build models that rival the U.S. but cannot serve them at scale.

But Beijing cannot ignore the urge to securitise and restrain access for too long. Sooner or later, it will face that choice. Indeed, Beijing is already reported to be weighing restrictions on overseas access to its most capable models.

One can expect China’s frontier models to move towards proprietary models, while trailing models remaining open-weight. But so long as China cannot overcome the larger constraint, it is likely to continue fielding open-weight models. And China’s constraint is the world’s gain.

#90. Want to get ahead at work? This behavioural psychologist turns career challenges into games

From discovering your strengths to creating opportunities, networking and navigating workplace politics, Ritika Datta turns career challenges into games. She tells CNA Women how female-led communities helped her get her start in Singapore’s diversity, equity and inclusion space.

Ritika Datta is a behavioural psychologist who creates games for learning in Singapore’s diversity, equity and inclusion space. (Photo: Felly Loi)

When Ritika Datta founded Hakopi in 2023 to create diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) games, she reached out to female-led communities to find out if they wanted to feature her games at their events.

The behavioural psychologist had just moved to Singapore with her husband. Having previously built games for adult learners, she wanted to continue her work.

Her first event was co-organised by Moxy, a platform that drives structural change for women in the workplace, and Lean In Network, Singapore, a non-profit that connects women and empowers them to reach their career goals.

“It spiralled from there – in a good way,” she said. Of the 200 women in attendance, many were interested in bringing her to their organisations.

“The women’s community was just so much easier for me to get into, even from a networking perspective, because they were very warm and open to having conversations and trying out new things,” the 34-year-old told CNA Women.

The first two game sessions she ran were, coincidentally, women-centric. This became a “niche” for Hakopi and her portfolio grew from there.

Datta’s games come in two buckets. The first are “games that change the world”. These aim to change the culture and how people work, tackling issues like belonging and allyship. The second are games to help you thrive in today’s world, teaching skills such as networking or getting more visibility in your career.

“If you have to wait for the world to become a better place before you do anything, you’re going to be waiting forever, so it has to work hand in hand,” she said.

One of the first games Datta created was Serendipity, about creating and grabbing opportunities.

“We believe – especially in the Asian context – that if you put your head down and work really hard, good things will come to you,” Datta said. “But as you move forward in life, you’ll realise that’s not quite true.”

She added: “We talk about this concept of what I call the surface area of serendipity. There are opportunities all around you, so if you’re sitting at your desk, you only have access to the opportunities around your desk.

“But the more you put yourself out there in meaningful ways, the more your surface area of serendipity increases and the more likely these opportunities are to find you.”

Players play in teams and step into the shoes of a particular character, making decisions for them to expand their surface area of serendipity.

Another game Datta created is Power Play, which gets players to recognise that workplace politics is not a dirty concept but a skill they can use to influence others. They play a product manager who needs to navigate internal politics and influence key stakeholders to get an idea approved.

“This game has a lot more debate and it’s fun because it’s so deeply rooted in us that I shouldn’t have to do all these extra things to get my work recognised,” said Datta.

Then there is Strength To Strength, a detective-themed game where players uncover their own strengths and learn how to communicate them with confidence.

“It gives people the tools to discover their own strengths and also realise how to fight back the inner voices – or sometimes also the external voices – that are telling them that’s not something you’re good at,” said Datta.

Although she collaborates often with female-focused communities, such as Moxy, The Elevate Group, Lean In Network, Singapore and Young Women’s Leadership Connection, most of her 15 flagship games can be played by both men and women.

Clients, which have included Google, LVMH, Macquarie Group and Marriott International, can take existing games or ask to modify stories or language to fit the company or industry.

Some may commission her to build a game that focuses on a particular issue – for example, she’s building one on male allyship.

Datta describes the process of developing a game as “a bit of a structured science”. She starts by looking at the learning needs of the topic through research and discussions with the target audience.

She takes inspiration from commercial board games when deciding on mechanics, such as selecting different cards or moving through different zones on a board.

“Once the mechanics are in place, I build the overarching story because you want players to feel like this is something they can relate to,” she explained.

It takes the Indian national around a month to create a new game. She runs Hakopi by herself but sometimes gets help with visual design work.

In September 2025, Datta conducted an introduction to finance workshop for about 200 migrant labourers.

The construction workers learnt about the relational aspects of budgeting, such as how to have a conversation with someone from home asking for money, how to tell them how much they can send and what they have to keep for themselves.

“Language was a problem as we had five different nationalities, with minimal English,” she recalled. “That was an interesting challenge, to make the language simple but teach complex concepts they could relate to.

“We also had to figure out how to run a game at a construction site during their lunch break. Luckily, they had a good time because it was different, counting fake money and throwing game chips at each other,” she added.

Datta said she hasn’t come across many others doing corporate workshops in the gender game space.

“The idea of using games for learning is fairly new, so you want to make sure that everyone in this space succeeds so that eventually you will succeed too.

“It’s a great community that has enough space for all of us to not feel like we’re stepping on each other’s toes,” she said.

Datta hopes that participants remember what they’ve taken away from her games.

“I don’t want them to comment on how the character was so well written or that the game design was amazing,” she explained. “I want them to say, ‘I didn’t realise that I was doing thing A when I should be doing thing B.’ That’s my internal measure of success."

She also wants to help people realise they are empowered to make changes, and that change doesn’t have to be a massive thing that’s scary and unattainable – it can be small steps.

“I want the games to show people that you have the power to change,” Datta said. “Even changing a small behaviour will have a cascading effect.”

#97. Trump's green card curbs test Indian tech workers' American dream

Indian tech workers are facing growing uncertainty amid US immigration curbs

For millions of Indians, the road to the ultimate career aspiration had a familiar script - an engineering degree to get a tech services job, then a posting in the US, repeated visa renewals and then the green card that gave them permanent residency in the country.

Earlier this month, the US announced it would suspend eight technology firms, including Microsoft, India's Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro and HCL Technologies, from hiring skilled international workers on a permanent basis through a widely used visa pathway.

New and pending applications under the Permanent Labor Certification Program, or PERM, have been frozen. This is a big blow to Indians who hold a majority of H-1B visas - a temporary visa for specialists - which they used to work in the US while relying on PERM to get a green card and, ultimately, citizenship.

The latest announcement is in line with Trump's push for stricter skilled-worker immigration rules, which tech firms have been adapting to and, therefore, the impact on businesses may be minimal, experts say.

Nasscom, the trade association of India's $300bn (Â£226bn) technology industry, says that Indian companies have reduced the dependence on H-1B visas and are hiring more locally.

"Consequently, the number of employees transitioning from H-1B visas to permanent residency through the PERM process is also relatively limited," the association adds.

Wipro said in a statement that more than 80% of its workforce in the US is locally hired. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), said its PERM applications were in single digits in the last two years. Microsoft said in a statement that it has filed for H-1B visas mostly to retain existing staff, not to import new workers.

A report in Business Standard newspaper, citing PERM Tracker, said, TCS' applications filings fell to two in 2025-26 from 513 in the previous year and 1,804 in the year before that. The report said no records were found for Infosys and Wipro for 2025-26.

BBC's email to all eight companies asking questions about the impact of the restriction and how they were supporting affected employees in the US remained unanswered.

Brokerage firm HDFC Securities said the impact on tech firms would be minimal, as PERM-linked jobs make up only a small share of revenue, even if higher-paid American workers replace them.

But the US government's decision has an impact beyond business.

Vice President JD Vance's remarks on H-1B workers added to concerns over US immigration policy

A US-based senior executive with one of these eight firms told the BBC on condition of anonymity that the new PERM restriction will "not break the business", but the impact is more "intense" at a human level.

"A good number of people we know are stuck in a space where they have spent some years in the US but their green card application hasn't moved. Even if they are ready to move back to India, their children don't want to because they grew up here," he said.

Recent policy moves have deepened uncertainty and amplified online hostility towards Indian immigrants and H-1B visa holders.

Researchers at Stop AAPI Hate, a US non-profit that combats anti-Asian and Pacific Islander discrimination, have reported a sharp increase in anti-South Asian online sentiment since the 2024 US election, with Indian immigrants and H-1B visa holders being frequent targets.

While barring the eight tech companies from PERM, US Vice President JD Vance too referred to foreign professionals holding H-1B visas as "foreign indentured servants".

While he later acknowledged that it was an "imperfect" rhetoric, his remark drew sharp criticism from India's foreign ministry which called it "unwarranted" and "deeply offensive" for the colonial connotation.

Poorvi Chothani, founder and managing partner of global immigration law firm LawQuest, said many came to the US for better prospects, borrowing heavily to study and build careers. But as rules tighten now, they have fewer options and less ability to absorb the costs.

Immigration experts believe that as pathways for residency become limited, some may reconsider their future in the US, while others may choose not to move at all.

Ahmet Gulek, Postdoctoral Fellow at Stanford's Immigration Policy Lab, told BBC that despite alternate ways to get residency, not many may be feasible.

While moving to another firm not covered by the suspension is an option, he flags that it's unclear if those firms would be open to hiring immigrants given "the fear of retaliation".

Besides, it's also a tough time to change jobs.

Indians dominate the US employment green card backlog

The technology industry has already been reshaped by layoffs, cost-cutting and the rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI), making career moves tougher.

But there are other routes to a US green card. People with exceptional accomplishments or whose work advances national interests may be eligible for categories that bypass the PERM labour certification process.

Applicants can also qualify for permanent residency by investing at least $800,000 in eligible US enterprises.

Gulek warns some of these categories "may be targeted next."

While Chothani says some are so keen to stay in the US that they are considering the EB-5 investor route but may not have enough capital to invest.

Nearly one million Indian nationals were waiting for employment-based green cards as of December 2025, representing 79% of the total pending applications, according to the National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP).

Their report projects wait times ranging from five to 179 years for highly skilled Indians filing labour certification or employment-based immigration petitions from January 2026, depending on the visa category.

Like other recent immigration measures, the PERM suspension may face federal court challenges. But together, these moves signal a more restrictive climate for those hoping to study, work or settle in the US.

Earlier, the Trump administration imposed a $100,000 fee on certain new H-1B visa petitions, a measure that was later struck down by federal courts. More recently, it has also proposed a $70,000 fee for universities recommending international students for a programme that allows graduates to work in the country after completing their studies.

"This trend is not changing anytime soon," said an Indian professional, who spoke to BBC on condition of anonymity and has transitioned to an H-1B visa recently after a "long wait". He says both the Republican Party and the Democratic Party agree immigration laws need reform, even if they disagree on how.

Some research suggests that restrictions could shift jobs to India rather than to American workers. This is supported by industry players saying that they are already witnessing an increase in companies offshoring work.

"Companies benefit from cost arbitrage when they move work offshore. Post Covid, there is also greater acceptance and appreciation for remote work from clients," the executive from the tech firm earlier quoted says.

Experts believe America's appetite for Indian tech talent is unlikely to disappear. What may be changing is how it taps that talent. For Indian professionals, that could mean rewriting the script about building a future in the US.
